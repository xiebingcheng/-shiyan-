> 「Grain Buds is the mid-node of the fourth month. 'Grain Buds' means that things have come thus far and stand slightly filled, not yet full.」 (「小满，四月中。小满者，物至于此小得盈满。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. Almost Full, Not Yet Full — the Wheat's Autumn Draws Near

**Grain Buds** (*Xiaoman*) comes after Beginning of Summer, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 60°; the node falls each year on 20 or 21 May of the Gregorian calendar. Eighth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — it takes a name that is nearly plain description: the wheat kernels have filled with sap and swollen plump, yet the day when they harden into ripe fullness has not arrived — *xiao de ying man*, "slightly filled": it names the very breath of the crops at almost-full, not-yet-full.

The old saying runs, "Grain Buds, Grain Buds — the rivers and ponds fill day by day." The north watches the wheat; the south watches the water: in Jiangnan, the country south of the lower Yangtze, the rivers and ponds are rising, right into the busiest stretch of wheeling water onto the fields. The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — write this flush of life in fine detail:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The bitter herbs blossom** (苦菜秀) | 「Master Bao says: touched by the qi of fire, the bitter flavor is formed.」 (「鲍氏曰：感火之气而苦味成。」) As the fire qi warms day by day, the bitterness of the wild bitter herbs comes just in season |
| **Second** | **The delicate grasses die** (靡草死) | 「Zheng Kangcheng and Bao Jingxiang both say: the *mi* grass is of the flixweed kind. The commentary on the *Book of Rites* says: it is the grass whose branches and leaves are soft and fine.」 (「郑康成、鲍景翔皆云：靡草，葶苈之属。《礼记注》曰：草之枝叶而靡细者。」) Fine, tender grasses born of yin cannot hold out under the strengthening sun |
| **Third** | **The wheat's autumn arrives** (麦秋至) | 「Autumn is the season in which the hundred grains ripen. Though by the calendar it is still summer, for the wheat it is already autumn; hence one speaks of the wheat's autumn.」 (「秋者，百谷成熟之期。此于时虽夏，于麦则秋，故云麦秋也。」) For the wheat, the autumn of harvest has arrived |

Grain Buds' folk customs all turn about the "Three Carts" (*san che*) — the saying runs *Xiaoman dong san che*: at Grain Buds, the three carts set to work. Gu Lu of the Qing, in the fourth volume of his *Qing Jia Lu* (《清嘉录》, *A Record of the Year's Pure Joys*), records an old custom of Suzhou:

> 「Grain Buds has but newly come; the silkworm women boil the cocoons, set the reels in order, and unwind the silk, working day and night.」 (「小满乍来，蚕妇煮茧，治车缫丝，昼夜操作。」)

The silk reel spins the silk, the oil press crushes the rapeseed, the waterwheel lifts water to the fields — no idle hands in the farm household. In Jiangsu and Zhejiang, Grain Buds was also taken as the birthday of the silkworm goddess, and a festival of prayer for the silkworms was held. Of the twenty-four solar terms, Lesser Heat is answered by Greater Heat and Lesser Cold by Greater Cold; Grain Buds alone is followed by no "Greater Fullness" (「大满」) — what is too full runs over, and it is the almost-ripe, not-yet-ripe that is the best stretch of the year's days.

For health, two things likewise come riding on this "gradual filling": the moisture fills, and the damp comes to collect its debt from the body; the fire fills, and heart fire pushes upward. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Elucidation of the Five Qi" (《素问·宣明五气》), says of the five flavors that 「the bitter enters the heart」 (「苦入心」) — to eat the bitter at this node is exactly seasonable.

## II. Nourishment: A Pot of Bean-and-Poria Soup, a Plate of Cold-Dressed Bitter Herbs

The damp's account calls for a reckoning. The Rain Water essay taught stir-fried Job's-tears water; the Grain Rain essay brought out adzuki bean soup with stir-fried Job's tears and aged tangerine peel — both aimed chiefly at drawing off water. By Grain Buds, drawing off water alone no longer suffices: the damp has squatted on the body through two solar terms and has begun to turn to heat — the tongue coating is yellower and greasier than before, and one grows easily heavy and drowsy. This pot of soup changes the approach: poria (*fuling*) and white hyacinth bean (*biandou*) fortify the spleen out front, and winter melon clears heat and drains water behind — a steadier line than one fierce draining ingredient alone.

**White hyacinth bean, poria, and winter melon soup**: 30 g of white hyacinth bean, 15 g of poria, 500 g of winter melon with the skin on, and three slices of ginger. Rinse the beans clean and soak them two hours; break the poria into small pieces and soak it in cold water half an hour; scrub the winter melon clean, skin and all, and cut it into large chunks — the skin is the part that draws off water, so do not peel it away. Put the beans and the poria into the pot with their soaking water and add about 1,200 ml more; bring to the boil over high heat, turn down to a low simmer, and cook forty minutes; once the beans have split and bloomed, add the winter melon and the ginger slices and cook fifteen minutes more, until the soup turns milky white, and season with a little salt. Drink it alongside the main meal, two or three times a week. Both ingredients are mild and level; hyacinth beans that are not cooked through readily cause bloating — be sure to cook them until they split open — and those who are yin-deficient, with dry mouth and a red tongue with scant coating, should drink little of it.

Bitter herbs (*kucai*) are Grain Buds' true seasonal green — first pentad, "the bitter herbs blossom," speaks of precisely this plant. The *Classic of Poetry* (the *Book of Odes*), in "Gu Feng" of the Bei Feng section, asks, 「Who says the *tu* is bitter? It is sweet as the shepherd's purse.」 (「谁谓荼苦？其甘如荠」) — and the *tu* of that line is the bitter herb. Li Shizhen of the Ming, in the "Vegetables" section (菜部) of his *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*), records its nature and flavor as 「bitter, cold, and without toxicity」 (「苦，寒，无毒」), and for its indications quotes the older texts (the "perverse qi" of the old texts is what we would call pathogenic influences; the *zang* are the five solid organs of Chinese medicine): 「It clears the perverse qi of the five *zang* organs, the oppression that spoils the appetite for grain, and the impediment of the stomach; taken long, it steadies the heart and boosts the qi, quickens the senses and lessens sleep, lightens the body and holds age at bay.」 (「五脏邪气，厌谷胃痹。久服安心益气，聪察少卧，轻身耐老」) The garden-grown kind is called endive — 「in truth, one and the same thing」 (「实一物也」), says the same entry — so when the market has no bitter herbs, endive and its curled cousin are of the same stock: substitute without worry.

**Cold-dressed bitter herbs**: 200 g of bitter herbs; pick away the old roots and yellowed leaves, soak ten minutes in lightly salted water, and rinse clean under running water. Bring a pot of water to the boil and drop them in; blanch thirty seconds — the moment the color turns deep green, lift them out at once — pass them through cool boiled water, squeeze dry, and cut into sections. Pound two cloves of garlic to a paste; add one spoonful of black vinegar, half a spoonful of light soy sauce, a small pinch of salt, and a few drops of sesame oil; pour it over, toss well, and let it stand five minutes before eating — only then does the garlic's pungence work its way in. In the city, wild bitter herbs can be hard to buy; half a bitter melon will stand in — slice thin, salt for ten minutes, and squeeze out the water, dress cold by the same method, or scramble with eggs. Those whose spleen and stomach are weak and cold, whose digestion turns loose at the first cold mouthful, should blanch a little longer, take less, and stop at a taste.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: When Damp-Heat Surfaces, the Skin Knows First

Once the days turn sultry, the skin is the first to make a fuss. A child's neck creases break out in a scatter of red dots; an adult's elbow creases and shins itch over and over — mostly prickly heat (miliaria) and eczema. Damp wraps heat and steams outward through the sweat pores; when sweat cannot pass freely, these troubles brew under closed skin. The *Suwen*, in its "Great Treatise on the Utmost Truth and the Major Essentials" (《素问·至真要大论》), lays the root bare in one sentence:

> 「All pain, itching, and sores belong to the heart.」 (「诸痛痒疮，皆属于心。」)

Sore and itch are charged to heart fire — and to damp-heat as well; treat along both lines at once. Prickly heat: bathe in plain water a degree or so below body temperature; after washing, pat the neck, the armpits, and the backs of the knees dry; wear loose clothing of pure cotton. Once the heat rash has come out, do not pile on thick moisturizer — a thin layer of calamine lotion quiets the itch; if it has not settled in two or three days, or has been scratched open, go to the hospital. Eczema: no scalding rinses — the hotter the water, the fiercer the itch, and the fiercer the itch, the more the hand wants to scratch. Take the alcohol, the spice, and the fried and sweet-greasy dishes off the menu first, and let mung bean soup take the iced drink's place. In skin matters, prevention comes first: wipe the sweat as it comes, change clothes the moment they are soaked, and do not muffle the body up.

Do not overlook heart fire's stirrings either: a sore on the tongue, nights of tossing and turning, urine gone dark. The Beginning of Summer essay opened the subject of nourishing the heart; at Grain Buds one more note must be added to it — nourishing the yin. Sweat is the fluid of the heart, and sweat streaming down in floods spends heart yin fastest of all. Move exercise to morning and evening and stop once a light sweat shows; fifteen minutes of closed eyes after lunch shakes off fatigue better than an iced Americano. Bitter-tasting greens and lotus-seed plumules steeped in water are helpers close at hand.

## IV. Daily Life: Lie Down at Night, Rise Early — and Do Not Hurry to Chase the Cool

The *Suwen*'s "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》) fixes the regimen for the three months of summer as 「lie down at night and rise early; do not weary of the long days」 (「夜卧早起，无厌于日」): in bed before half past eleven at night, up around six, and at noon a quarter of an hour to half an hour of catch-up sleep. "Lie down at night" here means sleeping somewhat later than in spring — not an excuse to scroll the phone deep into the night.

The air conditioners are taking up their posts; hold fast to a few rules: temperature no lower than 26 °C; the vent never pointed straight at anyone; and anyone who comes in drenched with sweat dries off before stepping into the cooled room. In the south, this term's trouble is the damp — quilts and pillows clench moisture in their grip, and mites and molds work through the night. Seize a clear day to sun the quilts and tuck two packets of desiccant into the wardrobe; that is worth more than any spray. After rain the mornings and evenings turn cool; keep carrying a thin jacket when you go out, as before. When cool wind burrows into a sweat-soaked back, a cold arrives faster than in winter.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Mulberries**. 「The hares hide where the bean shoots run fat; the birds call where the mulberries have ripened」 (「兔隐豆苗肥，鸟鸣桑椹熟」) — Bai Juyi of the Tang, in his *Mengxia Si Weicun Juji Ji Shedi* (《孟夏思渭村旧居寄舍弟》, "In Early Summer, Thinking of the Old Home at Wei Village, Sent to My Younger Brother"), wrote of precisely this season. Wash the purple-black, fully ripe fruit gently under running water, snip off the stems, soak five minutes in lightly salted water and rinse; eat them as fruit or fold them into yogurt. Thin-skinned and heavy with juice, they must be finished the same day — they will not keep. They nourish yin and engender fluids, just right for the dry mouth of early summer; those with loose stools should eat sparingly.

**Loquats**. The loquat is at its best in the fifth month; its flesh moistens the lung and engenders fluids, and peeled and pitted, eaten as it comes, it is the simplest refreshment of all. The seed carries the smell of bitter almond; do not chew it in play — spit the whole seed out. On days when the throat is dry and a cough sounds once or twice, double-boil the flesh with rock sugar — the covered bowl set inside a pot of boiling water — for twenty minutes, and take fruit and broth together.

**Mung bean sprouts**. The mung bean, allowed to sprout, clears heat and drains the damp without the bean's own cold that burdens the stomach — the cheapest damp-dispelling vegetable of the season. Quick-fry one minute over a high flame and splash a few drops of vinegar down the side of the wok — crisp, and not collapsed; they pair as well with Chinese chives as with glass noodles. For bagged sprouts, look to the root hairs: buy less of those that are rootless yet stretched very long.
