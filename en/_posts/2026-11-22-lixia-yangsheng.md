> 「Beginning of Summer is the node of the fourth month. For the word 'li' see the explanation given under spring. 'Xia' means grandness: by this time all things have grown grand and full.」 (「立夏，四月节。立字解见春。夏，假也，物至此时皆假大也。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. What the Term Means: "Xia" Means Grandness

**Beginning of Summer** (*Lixia*, 立夏) comes after Grain Rain, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 45°; the node falls each year on 5 or 6 May of the Gregorian calendar. Seventh of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — it is here that summer begins. The Grain Rain essay closed the spring run with 「spring comes thus far and takes its leave here」 (「春至此而别」) — and the word that takes up the baton now is this very "li": as the Beginning of Spring essay explained, 「what departs passes on, and what arrives takes up the thread」 (「往者过、来者续」). Spring's birth is gathered in; summer's growth opens.

"Grandness" (*jia*) here is not the *jia* of true and false; it means "great": all things, riding a yang qi (the season's warm, active energy) that strengthens by the day, grow toward their full measure — green to the very depths of green, stature stretched to the last inch. The *Book of Rites*, in its "Monthly Ordinances" chapter (《礼记·月令》), records for the first month of summer 「the mole crickets chirp, the earthworms come out, the king's melon sprouts, and the bitter herb comes into flower」 (「蝼蝈鸣，蚯蚓出，王瓜生，苦菜秀」); the three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — set against the commentary of the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*, run as follows:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The mole crickets chirp** (蝼蝈鸣) | 「The *louguo* is a small creature that burrows in the earth and likes to come out by night — the 'earth dog' people call it today, that is, the mole cricket... The *Huainanzi* says: when the mole crickets chirp and the earthworms come forth, the yin qi has begun, and these two creatures answer it.」 (「蝼蝈，小虫，生穴土中，好夜出。今人谓之土狗是也……《淮南子》曰：蝼蝈鸣，丘螾出，阴气始而二物应之。」) |
| **Second** | **The earthworms come out** (蚯蚓出) | 「The earthworm is the 'earth dragon'... The *Li Jie* — the *Explanations of the Calendar* — says: what belongs to yin and lies bent rides the yang and stretches itself out into view.」 (「蚯蚓即地龙也……《历解》曰：阴而屈者，乘阳而伸见也。」) |
| **Third** | **The king's melon sprouts** (王瓜生) | 「The *Tu Jing* — the *Illustrated Classic* — says: the king's melon is found everywhere, growing in the open wilds, in fields and homesteads and along walls... green while it grows, red when ripe.」 (「《图经》云：王瓜处处有之，生平野、田宅及墙垣……生青熟赤。」) |

The earth sends up one movement after another: small creatures of the mole-cricket kind begin to call in the dark, earthworms turn up out of the ground, and the king's melon — a climbing gourd of the wild field-edges, not the cucumber of the vegetable plot — throws out its vines and sets fruit.

Yet, as at Beginning of Spring, Beginning of Summer is only the messenger: the sun bites a little harder each day, the morning and evening winds still carry a coolness, and a gap of ten degrees or so between day and night is ordinary. In Jiangsu and Zhejiang the day turns on "seeing the three fresh things": the *Qing Jia Lu* (《清嘉录》, *A Record of the Year's Pure Joys*), in a note of its commentary, quotes the *Kun–Xin Gazetteer* (《昆新合志》, the combined gazetteer of Kunshan and Xinyang): 「The household sets out cherries, green plums, and wheat silkworms — young wheat kernels pressed into silkworm-shaped strands... — and the custom is named 'Beginning of Summer: seeing the three fresh things.'」 (「家设樱桃、青梅、麦蚕……名曰立夏，见三新。」) Livelier still is **weighing the people** (*cheng ren*), which the same book records in its fourth volume: 「Every household weighs its members on the great steelyard, and weighs them again at the Beginning of Autumn, to check how fat or lean the summer has left them.」 (「家户以大秤权人轻重，至立秋日又秤之，以验夏中之肥瘠。」) Old and young take turns on the stool hung from the great scale, and when Beginning of Autumn comes they climb up once more, to see whether the summer has told on them — the check being precisely against *zhuxia* (疰夏, the "summer ailment").

## II. Nourishment: Beginning-of-Summer Eggs and Broad Beans in Scallion Oil

At the Beginning-of-Summer table the lead role belongs to an egg — the **Beginning-of-Summer egg** (*lixia dan*). The folk proverb says, "eat an egg at Beginning of Summer, and no summer ailment will touch you in the hot days," and Jiangnan — the country south of the lower Yangtze — adds, "hang an egg on the chest at Beginning of Summer, and the child will not fall ill with *zhuxia*": weave a little pouch from colored thread and hang the egg at the chest, and when the egg-knocking game is done, off comes the shell and down goes the egg. Why the egg, of all things? Li Shizhen of the Ming, in his *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*), under the entry for the hen's egg in the "Birds" section, says:

> 「The egg-white images heaven: its qi is clear, its nature faintly cold. The yolk images earth: its qi is full and mellow, its nature warm. The egg takes white and yolk together, and so its nature is level.」 (「卵白象天，其气清，其性微寒；卵黄象地，其气浑，其性温；卵则兼黄白而用之，其性平。」)

One egg holds the clear and the turbid both, and lets cold and warmth temper each other — level in nature through and through. The old folk set it against the summer ailment for no other reason than this evenness.

**Five-spice Beginning-of-Summer eggs**: set eight eggs, about 500 g, in a pot of cold water; when the water boils, turn down to a low flame and cook eight minutes, lift them out to soak in cold water, and while they are still warm tap the shells all over into a fine, even crackle. In another pot bring 600 ml of water to the boil with 5 g of black tea, one star anise, a short piece of cinnamon bark, and one bay leaf; add 30 ml of light soy sauce, 10 ml of dark soy sauce, 6 g of salt, and 15 g of rock sugar, and let it boil until fragrant. Slip in the eggs, simmer a quarter of an hour more over a low flame, then turn off the heat and leave them steeping in the broth overnight; rewarm the next day and they are ready. Around Jiaxing the Beginning-of-Summer egg also takes in salted duck eggs — put down to cure before Pure Brightness and saved for this one day to be opened. One or two a day is plenty: the yolk carries no small amount of cholesterol, and those with high blood lipids should stop at a taste.

Beans are the other bite of freshness the day cannot forgo. The old saying goes, "beans in summer outdo meat": the new beans are tender, cleaner on the palate than the rich flavors of the season past. Jiangnan counts the broad bean among the day's "three fresh things of the earth" — shelled and eaten on the spot, for leave them one day more and a measure of the freshness is gone. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Grains" section, records the broad bean as 「sweet, faintly acrid, level, and without toxicity」 (「甘、微辛，平，无毒」), and quotes Wang Ying that it 「gladdens the stomach and harmonizes the organs」 (「快胃，和脏腑」) — just the thing for an appetite at the turn of the season.

**Broad beans in scallion oil**: shell 300 g of fresh broad beans; take five or six scallions, the whites cut in sections, the greens chopped fine. Heat 25 ml of oil in the wok and fry the scallion whites over a low flame until barely browned and fragrant; raise the heat, add the beans, and stir-fry two minutes. Season with 3 g of salt and 2 g of sugar, pour in 50 ml of hot water, cover, and simmer five or six minutes over a low flame — done when the skins wrinkle and the back of a spoon can crush them; scatter the greens over and serve, best of all with freshly cooked rice. For the larger beans, peel off the skins and keep the split halves, and the texture turns softer still. Two things to hold firm: first, stew them through and through — broad beans eaten raw or underdone are hard to digest; second, where there is a child with G6PD deficiency (favism) in the house, not a single bean may pass those lips.

Black rice (*wumi fan*) is on the Jiangnan list as well: pound the leaves of the southern candle into juice and steep glutinous rice in it, and the steamed rice comes out black and glossy; in Fujian and Zhejiang a potful goes on the steamer this day, in hopes of a safe season.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: Heart Fire Rising, *zhuxia* to Guard Against

The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Six Sections and the Visceral Manifestations" (《素问·六节藏象论》), says:

> 「The heart is the root of life, the dwelling of the spirit's transformation; its splendor shows in the face, its fullness lies in the vessels of blood; it is the great yang within yang, and it communicates with the qi of summer.」 (「心者，生之本，神之变也，其华在面，其充在血脉，为阳中之太阳，通于夏气。」)

The heart answers to the qi of summer, and from Beginning of Summer heart fire finds it easy to lift its head: nights spent turning from side to side, a tongue-tip gone red or even broken out in a small sore, a dry mouth and dark urine, a temper that flares at the least thing. Set the diet right first — push the heavy oils and the strong spice to the back of the queue; steep two or three lotus-seed plumules and a few leaves of lophatherum (dan zhuye, a mild bamboo-like herb) in hot water, and drink it before noon — those whose spleen and stomach run weak and cold should drink little of it. Soak the feet ten minutes before sleep, then knead Shenmen (HT7) and Neiguan (PC6) two or three minutes each — Shenmen sits in the hollow on the little-finger side of the wrist crease; Neiguan lies two *cun*, the body's own proportional inch, up from the middle of the wrist crease toward the elbow — and the heart-spirit settles back down.

The other thing to guard against is **zhuxia**: summer sets in, and the appetite fades, the limbs go slack, a low fever and lightheadedness come and go, and the person visibly thins — the old folk called it the "bitter summer," and the weighing of the people exists precisely to detect it. Prevention comes down to two small things: keep the food light, but do not stint on meals; and take the midday rest in full. If a lasting loss of appetite and a low fever do take hold, do not grit your teeth and carry on — go to the hospital first and have it looked into.

## IV. Daily Life: Lie Down at Night, Rise Early, and Add a Midday Nap

The *Suwen*'s "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》) sets the regimen for the three months of summer as 「lie down at night and rise early; do not weary of the long days」 (「夜卧早起，无厌于日」) — the schedule's clock moves one notch faster than spring's: asleep around eleven at night, up between five-thirty and six, and no begrudging the long daylight.

Of all the season's routines, the one Beginning of Summer presses hardest is the **midday nap**. At noon — the hours from eleven to one — the heart meridian is on duty; after a morning of gathering heat, drowsiness lands hardest in the early afternoon. After lunch, rest a quarter of an hour to half an hour: closing the eyes is enough, no need to sink into deep sleep — and where the office offers nothing better, a few minutes face-down on the desk still count. Even ten minutes with the eyes shut, and the afternoon temper runs far smoother.

Do not hurry the wardrobe into summer. The sun may be fierce, but mornings and evenings still hold a chill — carry a thin jacket about as before; leave the air conditioning off a while longer, and after sweating keep clear of the direct draft: with the pores standing wide open is just when the "thief wind" — the stray draft that slips in past an unguarded body — does its worst. Toward evening, while the air is cool, go out and walk briskly thirty or forty minutes, and let the qi stretch outward like the king's melon of the third pentad.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Amaranth**. A standing item on the Jiangnan Beginning-of-Summer table: stir-fry the red amaranth and it bleeds its red juice into the dish, and folded through rice it is "tasting the new" at its truest. Hot wok, little oil, two cloves of garlic crushed with the flat of the knife; two minutes over high heat and salt to finish — or drop a handful into soup or over rice porridge, which suits it just as well. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Vegetables" section, records the amaranth as 「sweet, cold and unblocking, and without toxicity」 (「甘，冷利，无毒」), and says that 「the six amaranths all free the large and small intestine」 (「六苋并利大小肠」) — its power to open the bowels is nothing slight: those whose spleen and stomach run weak and cold should take little, and in pregnancy it is not to be touched.

**Mulberries**. The purple-black berries of April and May, thin-skinned and heavy with juice; buy them the day they are sold and eat them the same day. Soak ten minutes in salted water, rinse, and eat as they are, or simmer them with glutinous rice into a congee; those who can bear the faint tartness may press them for juice and thin it with honeyed water. The *Bencao Gangmu*, under the mulberry fruit in its "Trees" section, records it as 「sweet, cold, and without toxicity」 (「甘，寒，无毒」), and quotes Chen Cangqi that eaten alone it 「stays the wasting-thirst, benefits the five zang organs and the joints, and frees the blood and qi」 (「单食，止消渴，利五脏关节，通血气」 — the five *zang* being the five solid organs of Chinese medicine). Cold in nature: those with a weak spleen and loose stools should stop at a taste.

**Loquats**. Around Jiangnan they say 「the loquats yellow in the fifth month」 (「五月枇杷黄」), and the skin peels away in a single pull. Choose the plump, orange-yellow ones and eat them peeled on the spot; where someone at home has a cough or a raw throat, loquats stewed with rock sugar will serve just as well. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Fruits" section, records the loquat fruit as 「sweet and sour, level, and without toxicity」 (「甘、酸，平，无毒」), and says of it that it 「stays the thirst, guides the qi downward, benefits the lung qi, and checks vomiting and retching」 (「止渴下气，利肺气，止吐逆」). The sugar is not low: those with high blood sugar should hold to one or two.
