> 「Frost's Descent is the mid-node of the ninth month. The qi is stern and congealing, and the dew has congealed into frost.」 (「霜降，九月中。气肃而凝，露结为霜矣。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. What the Term Means: The Qi Is Stern and Congealing, and Autumn Closes Its Books Here

**Frost's Descent** (*Shuangjiang*, 霜降) is the eighteenth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — and the sixth and last of autumn. Each year on 23 or 24 October of the Gregorian calendar the sun reaches celestial longitude 210° and the term begins; the lunar calendar sets it in the ninth month, and so the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* calls it 「the mid-node of the ninth month」 (「九月中」).

Frost does not fall from the sky. In today's terms, it is ground-hugging vapor that, chilled in the night, condenses straight into white ice crystals. But the four characters 「the qi is stern and congealing」 (「气肃而凝」) capture the very air of these days exactly: the sky presses down, the leaves press down, even the insects have gone quiet. The old saying runs that "the frost kills the hundred grasses," and after one night's white frost nearly all the green in the fields is gathered away; yet the spinach and radishes the frost has touched come out sweeter for it — low temperature drives the vegetables to turn their starch into sugar. In the north the radishes are just now being pulled and carried down into the cellars, as the farming proverb has it: "End of Heat brings the sorghum, White Dew the grain; when Frost's Descent arrives, pull the radishes." How to eat a radish is set out in full in the Lesser Snow essay, and there is no call to go over it here.

The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — have these fifteen days well filled:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The jackal offers up beasts in sacrifice** (豺乃祭兽) | 「'Sacrificing beasts' means taking the beasts and offering them in sacrifice to heaven, in repayment of the root; to lay them out in rows for the offering is the meaning of autumn's metal.」 (「祭兽，以兽而祭天，报本也。方铺而祭，秋金之义。」) The jackal catches its prey and lays it out as though making an offering |
| **Second** | **The grasses and trees turn yellow and shed** (草木黄落) | 「Yellow of color, and shaken to their fall.」 (「色黄而摇落也。」) |
| **Third** | **The hibernating insects all lie prostrate** (蛰虫咸俯) | 「'Xian' means all; 'fu' means to bow the head.」 (「咸，皆也；俯，垂头也。」) The insects all bow their heads into their burrows, neither eating nor stirring |

The Cold Dew essay left the dew one step from frost — that step, Frost's Descent has taken. Six solar terms of autumn close their books here, and the next station is Beginning of Winter. Brought down to the body, two accounts remain to be kept. One is tonification: the autumn harvest is nearly all in, and the body wants its base laid first. The other is moistening: the dryness has not gone out entirely — a dry cough and a dry mouth trail behind it, and the knees are the first to know.

## II. Nourishment: A Pot of Tomato-Stewed Beef Brisket, a Bowl of Sugarcane Congee

The old Minnan saying has it that "nourishing in winter is not so good as nourishing at Frost's Descent," and nowhere is it taken more to heart than in Yulin, Guangxi, which keeps the day as a festival in its own right — a bowl of beef-brisket rice noodles is that day's "nourishing." But the nourishing of Frost's Descent turns on one word: **level**. The *Suwen*'s "Treatise on the Viscera-Qi and the Four Seasons" (《素问·脏气法时论》) set out the frame for it long ago:

> 「The five grains serve for nourishment, the five fruits for aid, the five domestic animals for benefit, the five vegetables for filling; take them together, their qi and flavors joined, and they replenish the essence and boost the qi.」 (「五谷为养，五果为助，五畜为益，五菜为充，气味合而服之，以补精益气。」)

Grains, meat, fruits, and vegetables come in company, with no reliance on any one fierce ingredient; the drastic tonics — deer antler, aconite — are left for the deep-winter days of the 「counted nines」 (*shu jiu*, the nine nine-day cold counts after the winter solstice). What Frost's Descent wants is sweet warmth without dryness, coolness without cold.

**Tomato-stewed beef brisket**: 500 g of beef brisket, cut into cubes about 3 cm a side, set in a pot of cold water with two slices of ginger and a spoonful of cooking wine; once the scum floats, lift the meat out and rinse it clean. Four tomatoes, about 400 g in all: cut a small cross into the top of each, scald them half a minute in boiling water, and peel them; dice two and cut two into wedges. Heat a spoonful of oil in the pot and, over a low fire, fry the diced tomato until it melts down into a soft, sandy pulp; add the brisket, turn it two or three times, and pour in boiling water to cover it by two finger-joints. Transfer all to a clay pot and simmer over the lowest fire an hour and a half; add the tomato wedges and stew twenty minutes more, and stir in 3 g of salt ten minutes before it leaves the fire. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Beasts" section (兽部), quotes the *Mingyi Bielu* (《名医别录》, *Supplementary Records of Famous Physicians*) on beef: 「it settles the center and boosts the qi, and nourishes the spleen and stomach」 (「安中益气，养脾胃」), and Shizhen adds the Ming physician Han Mao's word that 「beef supplements the qi, doing the same work as astragalus」 (「牛肉补气，与黄芪同功」). In the pot this comes to a long, slow stew — stew until the meat comes apart at the first pinch of the chopsticks. For the old, give it another half hour; in an acute gout flare do not drink the thick broth, and those with high blood lipids should skim the fat off.

**Sugarcane congee**: one length of black-skinned sugarcane, about 300 g — peel it and cut it into short strips, add 600 ml of water, and simmer twenty minutes over a low fire; lift out the cane pulp and keep about 400 ml of the juice. Rinse 80 g of japonica rice clean, make the cane juice up to 800 ml with added water, and cook rice and juice together into a congee. No sugar is added — the sweetness is the cane's own. Wang Mengying's *Huichun Lu* (《回春录》, *Record of Returning Spring*) says of the cane that 「it is sweet and cool... with more than enough power to engender fluids」 (「蔗甘而凉……生津之力有余」), and names it 「a pulse-restoring decoction ready-made by nature」 (「天生复脉汤」). Boiling tones the coolness down a further step, and the dry mouth and dry tongue that deep autumn leaves over are just what this bowl of congee is there to finish off. For those with high blood sugar, this bowl is not the road — take the spinach road instead. Sugarcane whose pith has turned red, or that carries the smell of wine mash, has gone moldy: do not buy it.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: The Dry Cough at Its Close, the Knees That Fear the Cold

The cough first. A cough that has run from the Start of Autumn to now has mostly changed its temper on the way: before White Dew, phlegm yellow and sticky was warm dryness; after Cold Dew, phlegm white and thin with a dread of cold wind was cool dryness — the Cold Dew essay has written this out. By Frost's Descent what remains is mostly a dry cough without phlegm, an itchy throat, a spell or two of coughing after lying down at night — the dryness has reached its closing stretch, and only the word "dry" is left. Sip warm water, small mouthfuls, often, and do not wait for thirst and then gulp; keep up the pomelo honey of the Cold Dew essay, a spoonful melted in warm water before sleep to soothe the throat; and with the phlegm gone, do not be quick to move on to tonic pastes (*gaofang*, the thick herbal tonic pastes of winter) and heavy flavors — tonified too richly, the cough only drags out longer. A cough past four weeks that will not stop, phlegm streaked with blood, chest pain or fever — go to the hospital for a chest X-ray.

Then the knees. The moment the weather turns cold, the old cold-weather knee trouble reports for duty first: it aches and swells when the cool reaches it, and stiffens on the stairs. Choose knee supports of moderate elasticity, not so tight the leg goes numb; cyclists should add a windproof layer over the knees, for wind does the joints more harm than temperature does; warm up ten minutes before exercise; and at night wrap a hot towel in a layer of cloth and lay it on the knees for fifteen minutes. Joints that turn red, swollen, and hot, or morning stiffness that lasts past half an hour, must not be shrugged off as mere seasonal knee trouble — go to the rheumatology and immunology clinic and have them examined; this illness fears nothing so much as delay.

## IV. Daily Life: Out Only After the Frost Has Melted, with the Red Leaves at Their Height

The 「retire early and rise early」 (「早卧早起」) rule was copied out in full in the Start of Autumn essay — carry it out as written: in bed around half past ten. Frost forms in the night, and at dawn the ground is cold and slick; go out to exercise half an hour later than usual and wait for the sun to burn the frost off; frosted stone steps and frosted turf the old should walk around, not over.

For the spirits, the Autumn Equinox essay spoke of guarding against the autumn blues — the daylight shortens, and the mood sinks with it. And the outlet autumn leaves for the feelings is this last show of red leaves. In the north, go watch the smoke trees of the Fragrant Hills; in Jiangnan — the country south of the lower Yangtze — there are sweet gum and Chinese tallow; Yuelu Mountain and Tianping Mountain each keep a red of their own. Pick a clear day and go up after ten in the morning, a jacket along for the wind at the summit; those on the plain who cannot reach a mountain will find forty minutes of brisk walking around a park's ginkgo avenue does the same work. The leaves hold their red for two or three weeks only — miss it, and the wait is a year.

Two last things to close out the season: on a clear day, carry the autumn and winter quilts out to sun for two hours and bring them in after midday; and the long johns go on duty — autumn freezing (*qiu dong*, the autumn-side counterpart of spring covering) ends at Frost's Descent, the line the End of Heat essay drew, and to freeze on past it is only toughness for its own sake.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Persimmon**. The Minnan saying runs, "Eat a persimmon at Frost's Descent, and your nose will not run," and around Frost's Descent the astringency has faded clean out of the persimmons, which are now at their sweetest. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Fruits" section (果部), records the persimmon as 「a fruit of the spleen, the lung, and the blood aspect; its taste is sweet and its qi level, its nature astringent and able to bind and hold, and so it has the merit of fortifying the spleen and astringing the bowel, of treating cough and checking bleeding」 (「乃脾、肺、血分之果也。其味甘而气平，性涩而能收，故有健脾涩肠、治嗽止血之功」). The rules matter more than the praise. One a day is enough, and after meals. Never on an empty stomach — tannin meeting stomach acid readily congeals into a gastric phytobezoar — a hard lump the stomach cannot pass. Not in quantity at the same sitting as crab, fish, shrimp, and other high-protein foods — the Autumn Equinox essay has only just served the crab; keep some days between them. The crisp and sweet cultivars have already been de-astringed and may be eaten with an easy mind; for astringent persimmons, bag them up with apples for two or three days, or steep them a day in warm water. The skin holds the most tannin: do not eat it peel and all. Those with excess stomach acid, those who have had stomach surgery, and those with high blood sugar should stop at a taste.

**Spinach**. Spinach touched by frost comes in sweet and soft. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Vegetables" section (菜部), records the *boleng* — the old name for spinach — as 「it frees the vessels of blood, opens the chest and diaphragm, sends the qi downward and harmonizes the middle, quenches thirst and moistens dryness; the root is especially good」 (「通血脉，开胸膈，下气调中，止渴润燥。根尤良」) — the root, red as a beak, gave the folk the name "red-beaked, green-plumaged parrot," so do not pinch the roots off in washing. Its oxalic acid runs high: blanch it thirty seconds in boiling water before stir-frying or before it goes into soup. For those dry of mouth and dry of bowel, this mouthful fits; those loose of stool in the ordinary way should eat less of it.

**Olive**. The Fujianese call it the green fruit (*qingguo*); it comes to market in bulk around Frost's Descent, and chewed raw it is astringent first and sweet after, the sweetness returning at the back of the throat. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Fruits" section (果部), quotes the *Kaibao Bencao* on the olive: 「eaten raw or boiled into a drink, it dispels the toxins of wine in either way, and undoes the poison of the *tai* fish (mackerel)」 (「生食、煮饮，并消酒毒，解鲐鱼毒」). The home way: crack two or three fresh olives with the flat of a knife, stew them with a small piece of rock sugar for a quarter of an hour, and drink the liquid as water — when the throat is dry and itchy, it sits easier than a lozenge.
