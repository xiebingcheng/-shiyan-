> 「Grain in Ear is the node of the fifth month. It speaks of the bearded seeds — the grains may now be harvested and sown.」 (「芒种，五月节。谓有芒之种，谷可稼种矣。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. The Bearded Grains May Now Be Harvested and Sown

**Grain in Ear** (*Mangzhong*, 芒种) stands ninth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — and third of summer. Each year on 5 or 6 June of the Gregorian calendar, the sun reaches celestial longitude 75° and the term begins. The "mang" (芒) is the hard bristle on the husk of a wheat grain, and the tip of a rice sprout carries a fine one of its own; crops bearing awns, at this node, mean wheat to be harvested in haste and rice to be hurried into the ground. The farmers hear the name as "busy sowing": "Grain in Ear, grain in ear — reaping and planting in one breath," the busiest node of the whole year.

All three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — circle about 「the first stirring of a faint yin」 (「微阴初生」):

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The mantis is born** (螳螂生) | 「The mantis is a creature of the grasses... it lays its eggs deep in autumn among the woods and trees, a hundred offspring to one case, and at this time they break the case and come out.」 (「螳蜮，草虫也……深秋生子于林木间，一壳百子，至此时则破壳而出。」) The sangpiaoxiao of the Chinese pharmacopoeia — a medicine in its own right — is precisely this egg-case. |
| **Second** | **The shrike begins to cry** (鵙始鸣) | 「The *ju* is the shrike... the shrike cries in the fifth month, and its cry goes *ju-ju*; hence it was named for the sound.」 (「鵙，百劳也……百劳以五月鸣，其声鵙鵙然，故以之立名。」) |
| **Third** | **The mockingbird falls silent** (反舌无声) | 「The mantis and the shrike are both creatures of yin, stirred by the faint yin — the one to be born, the other to cry; the mockingbird is stirred by yang and finds its voice, and meeting the faint yin, it falls silent.」 (「螳螂、鵙皆阴类，感微阴而或生或鸣；反舌感阳而发，遇微阴而无声也。」) |

In the south, people keep up one more unhurried business at this node: boiling the plums. Around Grain in Ear the green plums ripen toward yellow; picked and boiled, they lose their astringency. In Chapter 21 of the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, when Cao Cao invites Liu Bei to a quiet drink, it is precisely this that stands on the table — 「a plate of green plums, a pot of warmed wine」 (「盘置青梅，一樽煮酒」). The famous talk of heroes over warmed wine is a novelist's invention, but the rain that falls when the plums yellow is heaven's own timetable: year after year the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze enter the plum rains (*meiyu*) in the first ten days or so of June, and once the yellow-plum rain sets in it may fall for half a month at a stretch. Zhao Shixiu of the Song caught it best: 「In the season of yellow plums, rain falls on every house; by the grassy pond, frogs cry on every side」 (「黄梅时节家家雨，青草池塘处处蛙」).

## II. Nourishment: A Jar of Preserved Green Plums, a Cup of Roasted-Rice Ginger Tea

In the plum-rain days the summer heat comes laced with damp; move a little and you are covered in sticky sweat. When sweat runs and thirst follows, the answer lies in "sour." Li Shizhen of the Ming, in the "Fruits" section of his *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*), records the plum: the smoked plum (*wumei*) is 「sour, warm, level, astringent, and without toxicity」 (「酸、温、平、涩、无毒」); Shizhen says the plum 「flowers in winter and ripens its fruit in summer, taking to itself the full qi of wood, and so its flavor is the sourest of all」 (「花开于冬而实熟于夏，得木之全气，故其味最酸」), and he lays bare the ailments the smoked and the white plum treat — 「all are taken in the sense of sour-gathering」 (「皆取其酸收之义」). Sour-gathering generates fluids, and sour joined with sweet transforms into yin — squarely the road to take when much sweat spends the body's fluids. The old rule at Grain in Ear is to boil and preserve the plums while they are fresh, and it is not hard to do at home:

**Rock-sugar preserved green plums**: 500 g of green plums, 200 g of rock sugar, one small spoon of coarse salt. Pick the stems out clean with a toothpick, rub the plums gently with the coarse salt to strip off the fuzz, and soak them two hours in clean water to draw the astringency; bring a pot of water to the boil, blanch the plums one minute, lift them into cold water to cool, drain, and leave them until quite dry. Scald a glass jar and let it dry; pack it to the top in layers — a layer of plums, a layer of sugar — seal it tight, and set it in the refrigerator; in three to five days the sugar melts and the plum juice seeps out, and after a week they are ready to eat. Spoon out two plums with a spoonful of the syrup, thinned with warm water or sparkling water as you like — one small cup a day, taken after meals. Two hard rules: the plum pit contains cyanogenic glycosides — do not bite into the whole pit, and pit the plums first for children; green plums are highly acidic — anyone with much stomach acid or a habit of reflux should cut the amount down, and never take them on an empty stomach.

At the other end of the muggy days lies the craving for cold: the iced drink goes down, and the spleen and stomach are the first to pay. The old saying runs, "eat radish in winter, ginger in summer" — ginger at midsummer warms the center and rouses the spleen. The materia medica details of ginger were given in full in the food-therapy column's essay *Shēngjiāng* (On Fresh Ginger); here only the eating matters:

**Roasted-rice ginger tea**: take 30 g of rice and stir-fry it in a dry pan over a low flame until golden and toasty-fragrant, then tip it out of the pan; while the pan still holds warmth, add 10 g of shredded ginger (skin on) and give it a turn or two, pour in about 800 ml of water, and simmer fifteen minutes — it is done when the aroma of the rice comes out. Those in a hurry may steep it twenty minutes in a vacuum flask. Drink it from morning through late morning; leave it alone after supper. It suits those best whose tongue coating is thick and white, who have no appetite in the morning, or who bloat at the least touch of cold food — and the belly is warm after drinking it.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: Muggy Damp, Heavy Sweating, Mildew, and Heatstroke

Damp first. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Vital Qi That Communicates with Heaven" (《素问·生气通天论》), describes how it feels when damp lays one low: 「when dampness is the cause, the head feels as if swathed」 (「因于湿，首如裹」) — the head as though wrapped in a wet towel. Waking heavy and sluggish, a sticky taste in the mouth, a thick greasy tongue coating, stools that cling to the bowl — any two of these, and the damp pathogen may be counted as having moved in. Push the iced drinks and the sweet, greasy dishes to the back of the line; keep kneading Yinlingquan (SP9), the point shown in the Grain Rain essay, and make the damp-dispelling soup by the Grain Rain recipe; those with a thick white coating and bloating at the least cold food should bring on the cup of roasted-rice ginger tea from Section II; and when the evening rain lets up, walk briskly half an hour to a light sweat — better than sitting shut in the house.

Sweat next. The *Suwen*'s "Treatise on the Elucidation of the Five Qi" (《素问·宣明五气》) says: 「the five zang transform into fluids — the heart's is sweat, the lung's nasal mucus, the liver's tears, the spleen's saliva, the kidney's drool」 (「五脏化液：心为汗、肺为涕、肝为泪、脾为涎、肾为唾」). Sweat is the heart's fluid; let too much of it out, and palpitations and lassitude follow close behind. Replenish water without gulping: a small half-cup at a time, spread evenly over the day, the water near body temperature; after a heavy sweat, a cup of warm green-plum juice is gentler than ice water. For those who sweat at the least motion, grab a small handful of floating wheat (*fuxiaomai*) and boil it along with the congee: the *Bencao Gangmu* records the floating wheat as 「benefiting the qi, dispelling heat, and checking spontaneous sweating and night sweats」 (「益气，除热，止自汗盗汗」).

The third business is mildew. Nothing outruns the mold in the plum-rain days: grains and flours go into sealed jars, and opened bags of nuts into the refrigerator; leftovers are finished the same day and heated through before eating; peanuts or corn that have turned rancid go out with the whole bag — mycotoxins withstand high heat, and no boiling kills them. Laundry that has not dried through is better tumble-dried before it is put away, and a pack of desiccant goes on the shelf inside the cabinet.

Last is summer heat. From Grain in Ear on, the sun has begun to turn vicious. Early heatstroke shows mostly as dizziness, thirst, pouring sweat, and legs gone soft. Move into the shade, loosen the collar, lay a wet towel on the back of the neck, and sip lightly salted water in small mouthfuls; if half an hour brings no easing, or confusion of mind appears, do not wait — get to a hospital.

## IV. Daily Life: Lie Down at Night, Rise Early — and Add to the Noon Rest

The *Suwen*'s "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》), giving the general rule for the three months of summer, says 「lie down at night and rise early; do not weary of the sun」 (「夜卧早起，无厌于日」), and 「let the qi have its outflow, as if that which you loved lay outside」 (「使气得泄，若所爱在外」). The days grow light early: up at half past five or six, no lying in; asleep before eleven at night. Clothes soaked with sweat are not worn again once they have dried on you — change them out. The air conditioner may be run in dehumidify mode, its airflow pointed away from you, at around 26 °C.

The noon rest must be raised a notch. The Grain Rain essay said to nap a quarter of an hour after lunch; from Grain in Ear on, take it up to twenty or thirty minutes — at the noon hour the heart meridian — the qi channel of Chinese medicine — is the one on duty, and with the day's sweat draining the heart's fluids, this nap is the heart's resupply. Past half an hour, though, the head turns groggier than before; set an alarm.

In the close weather tempers fray easily, and the four characters 「let the will be free of anger」 (「使志无怒」) do real work: when the fire rises, take a sip of warm water first and get up to walk a few steps. Do not pack the schedule full, either — that little leisure of plums and warmed wine is just what the busy-sowing days are meant to spare.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**New wheat**. Around Grain in Ear the North China Plain brings in its wheat, and "tasting the new" is the old rule. Rinse 50 g of wheat kernels and soak them two hours; add 50 g of japonica rice (the short-grain rice of everyday Chinese cooking), bring the water to a boil, turn the flame down low, and simmer forty minutes — done when the porridge thickens and the wheat aroma rises. Save the new flour for Summer Solstice noodles; for these few days, the wheat-kernel porridge comes first.

**Muskmelon**. The melons of the north and the pear melons (*ligua*) of Jiangnan come to market one after another; the "Fruits" section of the *Bencao Gangmu* records the flesh as 「quenches thirst and relieves vexing heat」 (「止渴，除烦热」). Choose a melon by smelling the blossom end — a full aroma means it has ripened well. The melon leans cold and slippery: after chilling, taste sparingly, let a cut melon come up to near room temperature before eating, and keep to half a small melon a day.

**Duck eggs**. Households in Jiangnan have an old custom of salting eggs when summer comes in, and the salted duck eggs on the Dragon Boat Festival table were mostly jarred a month before. Salting a jar now catches the next round: wash ten duck eggs and let them dry; roll each in strong white liquor (baijiu) and pack them into the jar. Boil 1,000 ml of water with 250 g of salt and let it cool; pour it in to cover the eggs, add 50 ml more of the white liquor, and seal for twenty-five to thirty days — when the yolks turn oily and sandy, open the jar around Lesser Heat (Xiaoshu, the term after next) and eat them with congee. On days of heavy sweat, half a salted duck egg with a bowl of plain congee holds you up better than plain water alone.
