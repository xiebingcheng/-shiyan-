> 「Grain Rain is the mid-node of the third month. Since Rain Water the rich earth has pulsed with life; now heaven rains upon the grain in the water. 'Yu' here is to be read in the departing tone (the falling tone of classical Chinese), as a verb — 'to rain upon,' as in 'let it rain upon my lord's common field.' For it is at this time that the grain is sown, and the rain comes down upon it from on high.」 (「谷雨，三月中。自雨水后，土膏脉动，今又雨其谷于水也。雨读作去声，如雨我公田之雨。盖谷以此时播种，自上而下也。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. Rain Begets the Hundred Grains — the Last Stop of Spring

**Grain Rain** (*Guyu*, 谷雨) comes after Qingming (Pure Brightness), when the sun reaches celestial longitude 30°; the node falls each year on 19 or 20 April of the Gregorian calendar. Sixth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — it is the last stop of spring. The spring run of this series writes its closing page here, and with it the frost calls a halt: the farming proverb says that Pure Brightness ends the snow, and Grain Rain ends the frost.

As for the name, the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* gives the clearest account: after Rain Water the fertile earth has pulsed awake, and the rain of this node no longer merely soaks the ground — it 「rains upon the grain in the water」 (「雨其谷于水」), falling into the grain that waits for the sowing, and the grain is born of the rain. The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — arrive trailing water vapor and farm work:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **Duckweed begins to grow** (萍始生) | 「The duckweed is a plant of the water; it keeps level with the water's surface, and so is called *ping*; it drifts wherever the wind goes, and so is also called *piao*.」 (「萍，水草也，与水相平，故曰萍。漂流随风，故又曰漂。」) |
| **Second** | **The cuckoo preens its wings** (鸣鸠拂其羽) | 「The dove here is the very one the hawk turns into — the bugu, the cuckoo. 'Fu' means to beat past, to brush against.」 (「鸠，即鹰所化者，布谷也。拂，过击也。」) Its call urges the ploughing on, true to the proverb "around Grain Rain, plant melons and sow beans." |
| **Third** | **The hoopoe alights on the mulberry** (戴胜降于桑) | 「The hoopoe is also named the *dairen*. The commentary on the *Erya* (China's oldest dictionary) says: it bears crest feathers on its head, and at this time it keeps always to the mulberries — the sign, it seems, that the silkworms are soon to hatch.」 (「戴胜，一名戴鵀。《尔雅》注曰：头上有胜毛，此时恒在于桑。盖蚕将生之候矣。」) |

The day keeps its various appointments across the country. Tea lovers wait for the pre-rain tea; flower lovers for the peonies — the peony is also known as the "Grain Rain flower," and the proverb bids one go look at the peonies three days into Grain Rain. In the northwest there was the old custom of *zou Guyu*, "walking the Grain Rain": a round of visits from village to village and kin to kin, or simply one long turn in the open country. And in Baishui, Shaanxi, offerings are made to Cangjie, the legendary maker of the written characters — for on the night he made them, the story goes in the *Huainanzi* (《淮南子》), 「the heavens rained grain and the ghosts cried through the night」 (「天雨粟，鬼夜哭」, 《淮南子·本经训》).

As for health, Grain Rain holds two things back for the finale: the damp, which grows heavier with every ten days and must be driven out; and the poplar and willow catkins, which have risen into the air and must be guarded against.

## II. Nourishment: A Cup of Pre-Rain Tea, a Pot of Damp-Dispelling Soup

Tea first. Xu Cishu of the Ming, in his *Cha Shu* (《茶疏》, *A Treatise on Tea*), under the entry "Plucking" (采摘):

> 「Pure Brightness and Grain Rain are the season for picking tea. Pure Brightness is too early, the Beginning of Summer too late; the days around Grain Rain are just right. And if you will bear to wait a day or two longer, until the leaf's vigor has come to fullness, the fragrance is doubly strong, and the tea keeps the better.」 (「清明谷雨，摘茶之候也。清明太早，立夏太迟，谷雨前后，其时适中。若肯再迟一二日期，待其气力完足，香烈尤倍，易于收藏。」)

Tea plucked before Pure Brightness is tender — and priced to match; the pre-rain leaf is the one with 「its vigor come to fullness」 (「气力完足」), and that is why Grain Rain tea became the most firmly rooted of the season's food customs. Brew it like this: 3 g of dry leaf spread across the bottom of a glass; water at 80 to 85 °C — boiling water dashed straight onto the leaf turns it bitter and harsh at once; pour in a third of the water first and swirl the cup a minute to warm and waken the leaf, then top up to seven-tenths. Drink it in the morning, and stop at two or three cups a day. Fresh green tea leans cool: no strong brews on an empty stomach; those whose stomach runs cold should switch to black tea or an aged tea, and anyone who values a night's sleep leaves it alone from the afternoon on. As for the proverb — "toon sprouts before the rain are tender as silk" — those who hanker after the taste may blanch the shoots and take a few chopsticks' worth and stop; the finer points of the toon were covered in the Spring Equinox essay.

Then the soup. The Rain Water essay taught stir-fried Job's tears steeped as a tea — enough against the light damp of early spring; by Grain Rain that lightness no longer suffices, and the adzuki bean must be called up to lead.

**Adzuki bean soup with stir-fried Job's tears and aged tangerine peel**: 30 g of adzuki beans, 20 g of stir-fried Job's tears (buy them ready-fried, or stir-fry them yourself as the Rain Water essay showed), and one small piece of aged tangerine peel, about 2 g. Pick the slender, elongated adzuki beans — the round, plump ones are kidney beans; for drawing off water it is the slender kind that does the work. Rinse everything clean; soak the adzuki beans two hours ahead, then set them on the fire in their soaking water and add about 1,200 ml more; bring to the boil over high heat, turn down low, and simmer forty minutes — done when the soup has turned red and the beans are on the point of splitting open; salt to taste. To serve it as a soup for the whole family, add two slices of ginger and stew it one hour with 250 g of blanched pork spine. Take it two or three times a week, alongside the main meal. The aged tangerine peel, acrid and fragrant, helps the digestion along and pulls the coolness of the Job's tears back a notch; both ingredients lean toward draining, so those who are yin-deficient, thin and dry, should not drink it every day — and Job's tears call for caution in pregnancy.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: Damp in the Body, Catkins in the Air

After Grain Rain, in the south the rains run together day upon day, and the damp comes to collect its debt from the body. The signals the Rain Water essay listed show themselves more often over these two weeks: waking with the body feeling leaden, a thick greasy tongue coating, sticky stools, calves tight and swollen. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Viscera-Qi and the Four Seasons" (《素问·脏气法时论》), puts the root of the matter in one sentence:

> 「The spleen suffers from dampness; hasten to feed it the bitter, that it may be dried.」 (「脾苦湿，急食苦以燥之。」)

The spleen cannot abide damp; when the damp runs heavy, the spleen is the first to suffer. Three roads of care. Hold the mouth: push the iced drinks and the sweet, greasy dishes to the back of the line — the sweet breeds damp and the cold bogs down the spleen — and let the damp-dispelling soup carry the front, two or three times a week. Get moving: a brisk walk toward evening, or one run-through of the Baduanjin — the "Eight Pieces of Brocade," the classic qigong sequence — stopping once a light sweat shows. And add one acupoint: Yinlingquan (SP9), just below the inner side of the knee, in the hollow along the inner edge of the shinbone; knead it two or three minutes a day.

The north carries one more trouble besides: the catkins. Mid- to late April is the very height of the drifting, and those with allergic rhinitis, conjunctivitis, or itching skin are struck in a concentrated burst. Wear a mask going out, and windproof goggles on the bike; the catkins are thickest on clear afternoons, so leave the airing of rooms to the morning, the evening, and the hours after rain; wash the face and rinse the nose on coming home; bring the drying laundry indoors — catkins spending a night on the quilt are the most trying of all. And those whose symptoms will not settle, or who feel the chest tighten and the breath go short, should not grit their teeth and bear it — go to the hospital. An allergy is not a thing to put off.

## IV. Daily Life: Lie Down at Night, Rise Early, and Put Spring Covering Away

The *Suwen*'s "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》) gives the general rule for the three months of spring — 「lie down at night and rise early; walk at ease in the courtyard」 (「夜卧早起，广步于庭」); the Beginning of Spring essay quoted the passage in full, and Grain Rain keeps to the same schedule: in bed before eleven at night, up around six in the morning, a fifteen-minute nap after lunch.

**Spring covering** (*chun wu*, the old rule of keeping the winter layers on well past the calendar's turn to spring) should close up its stall by Grain Rain. The Beginning of Spring essay gave the word — 「shed one layer at a time」 (「一重渐减一重」) — and the Spring Equinox essay was still warning not to hurry the long johns into retirement; now, this batch of long johns may at last retire. Folk experience takes 15 °C as the threshold: when the day's lowest temperature has held steady above 15 °C for three to five days running, and the mornings and evenings no longer chill the hands into the sleeves, begin putting the layers away, one by one. After Grain Rain the rains multiply, so keep a thin jacket about you when you go out. The covering was there to shelter the yang qi — the body's warm, active energy; its purpose served, it goes.

The old custom of *zou Guyu*, "walking the Grain Rain," transfers neatly to today: an hour after supper, choose the peonies' blooming days and walk forty minutes in the park — the flowers are the pretext, the walking is the business. This season's qi runs outward; do not shut it back in.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Mandarin fish** (*guiyu*). 「Peach blossoms drift on the flowing water, and the mandarin fish run fat」 (「桃花流水鳜鱼肥」) — Zhang Zhihe of the Tang, in his *Yu Ge Zi* (《渔歌子》, *A Fisherman's Song*), wrote of precisely this fish at the late-spring rising of the waters. The *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*), in its "Scales" section (鳞部), records the flesh as 「sweet, level, and without toxicity」 (「甘，平，无毒」), and Li Shizhen adds that it 「supplements the deficient and weary and benefits the spleen and stomach」 (「补虚劳，益脾胃」). Choose a live fish of about 500 g, make two shallow cuts along the back, and stuff them with shredded ginger and scallion sections, and steam eight minutes over high heat once the water boils; turn off the fire and let it stand two minutes in the trapped steam, then dress with steamed-fish soy sauce and finish with a spoonful of hot oil poured over. Few bones and thick flesh — a fish the old and the young can all manage.

**Elm samaras** (*yuqian*). A last fresh bite at the tail end of spring in the north — the winged seeds of the elm, round as little coins, which is where the name comes from. While they are young and green, wash and drain them, toss them with a thin coat of flour, steam five minutes over a high flame, and dip them in garlic juice and sesame oil — or knead them into the dough for *wowo*, the steamed cornmeal cones. The window is only ten days or so; once they age, a single gust of wind scatters them. The elms of parks and roadside plantings have mostly been sprayed; for eating, go to the vegetable market.

**Dandelion**. Pinch off the tender leaves and blanch them a minute or two to draw the bitterness, then dress them cold with black vinegar, salt, and sesame oil — or chop them fine and scramble them with eggs; whatever cannot be eaten in time, dry in the sun and boil up as a drink in summer. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Vegetables" section (菜部), records the young plant as 「sweet, level, and without toxicity」 (「甘，平，无毒」), and Shizhen says it 「resolves the poisons of food, disperses stagnant qi, and transforms heat's poison」 (「解食毒，散滞气，化热毒」). Those of weak and cold spleen and stomach should take little of it raw; blanch it first, then serve.
