> 「Lesser Snow, the middle node of the tenth month. The rain falls and, pressed by the cold qi, congeals into snow; "lesser" is the word for what is not yet abundant.」 (「小雪，十月中。雨下而为寒气所薄，故凝而为雪。小者，未盛之辞。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. The Rainbow Hides from Sight — Heaven and Earth Begin to Close Their Doors

Each year around 22 November on the Gregorian calendar, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 240°, **Lesser Snow** (*Xiaoxue*, 小雪) arrives — the twentieth of the twenty-four solar terms, the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. As for the "lesser" in the name, Wu Cheng puts it plainly: snow has shown itself, but not yet in earnest.

The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — grow colder one after another:

- First pentad, "the rainbow hides and is seen no more" (虹藏不见). The ancients held the rainbow to be born of the mingling of yin and yang qi; now, as the old gloss has it, "yin and yang have parted to their utmost" — yin in the ascendant, yang in decline — and the rainbow lurks hidden beneath the earth;
- Second pentad, "the qi of heaven ascends, the qi of earth descends" (天气上升，地气下降). Yang and yin each return to their own place and no longer meet;
- Third pentad, "closure and obstruction complete the winter" (闭塞而成冬). "What does not meet does not flow; what does not flow is shut fast" — heaven and earth seem to have closed their doors.

With the doors shut, people turn to pickling. In the north of China, families ferment sour cabbage and salt down jars of pickles, wiping the rims of their crocks scrupulously clean; in the south, before the hard frost sets in, strips of meat rubbed with salt and soy sauce go up under the eaves one by one. "Pickle the vegetables at Lesser Snow; cure the meat at Greater Snow." In the crocks and under the eaves, north and south are really doing the same thing: sealing up the last of autumn's harvest to be eaten slowly through the winter.

## II. The First of the Warm Tonics: **Danggui, Ginger, and Mutton Decoction**

From the *Jingui Yaolue* (《金匮要略》, *Essential Prescriptions from the Golden Cabinet*), Chapter 10, "On Abdominal Fullness, Cold Hernial Pain, and Retained Food" — **Zhang Zhongjing** (Han dynasty):

> 「For cold hernial pain in the belly, gripping and pulling at the ribs with an inward tightness: Danggui, Ginger, and Mutton Decoction is the remedy for it.」

The original formula — *Danggui Shengjiang Yangrou Tang* (当归生姜羊肉汤) — has just three ingredients: **danggui three *liang*, fresh ginger five *liang*, mutton one *jin*** — "take these three; add eight *sheng* of water, boil it down to three *sheng*, and take seven *ge* warm, three times a day" (the *liang*, *jin*, *sheng*, and *ge* are Han-dynasty units of weight and capacity). What Zhang Zhongjing treated was the belly pain of blood deficiency — a Chinese-medicine pattern in which the blood is too scant to nourish and warm the body — invaded by cold: the abdomen cramps with cold pain, the ache pulls toward the ribs, tightening in waves. The mutton warms the center and supplements the deficient, the danggui (Chinese angelica root) nourishes the blood, the ginger disperses cold. It is less a prescription than a pot of careful broth: in the *Jingui Yaolue*'s chapter on postpartum disorders, this same formula serves for the belly pain of new mothers whose depleted blood has taken in cold.

To make it at home, for a meal of three to four:

Mutton (leg or rib cuts), 750 g — start it in a pot of cold water with two slices of ginger and a spoonful of cooking wine; once the scum rises, lift the meat out and rinse it clean. Danggui, 10 g; ginger, 50 g — far gentler than the original proportions: the intent of dispersing cold is there, but you taste no heat. Put the danggui and ginger into a clay pot with the mutton, pour in plenty of hot water, bring it to a boil over high heat, then turn it down and simmer for an hour and a half; salt only in the last twenty minutes. When the soup has simmered to a milky white and the mutton comes apart at the first pinch of the chopsticks, it is done. The trick of the heat lies in that one word, "lesser": a hard boil leaves the soup cloudy and gamey; a small fire, left to simmer slow, makes it clear and sweet.

Some people should pass on this dish. Those with yin deficiency and smoldering fire — a red tongue with scant coating, heat in the palms and soles, mouth ulcers at the slightest trigger — Zhang Zhongjing warned of them long ago, and the *Jingui Yaolue* says it plainly: "As for mutton, those who harbor latent heat must not eat it." Set it aside, too, while a cold with fever runs its course, and for those of damp-heat constitution with a thick, greasy yellow coating on the tongue. For the healthy, once a week is enough: the virtue of warm tonification lies in frequency and small amounts, not in force.

## III. Daily Life: **Retire Early and Rise Late — Wait for the Sunlight**

The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit," calls the three months of winter the season of **"closing and storing"**:

> 「The three months of winter are called closing and storing: water freezes and the earth cracks; do not disturb the yang. Retire early and rise late — wait for the sunlight... Keep clear of the cold and seek the warm; do not let the skin leak and sweat, or the qi will be stripped away in haste. This is how to answer the qi of winter: the way of nourishing what is stored.」

In daily terms: go to bed an hour or two earlier than in summer, and do not head out to exercise in the dark — wait until the sun is up. Cold enters through the feet: before bed, soak the feet for fifteen minutes in water around 40 °C, deep enough to cover the ankles, just until the back feels faintly warm — do not soak yourself into a sweat; the more you sweat, the more the stored yang qi, the body's warm active energy, leaks away. Out of doors, a scarf guards the nape of the neck; keep shoes and socks dry and thick. However strong the heating indoors, open the windows for ten minutes each morning and evening to change the air — no one should sit sealed inside air that is both dry and hot.

## IV. Against the Gloom: Borrow a Little Sunshine from a Farmer of the Old State of Song

After Lesser Snow the days grow shorter day by day, and many people find themselves inexplicably flat and low — a seasonal reaction to the shrinking daylight, not a matter of willpower. The ancients had their own remedy for it: sunning the back, to which they gave an elegant name, **fuxuan** — "bearing the sun's warmth."

In the *Liezi* (《列子》, a Warring States-era collection of Daoist tales attributed to Lie Yukou), the "Yang Zhu" chapter tells of a farmer of the old state of Song who wears coarse hemp through the winter; out working in the early-spring sun, warmed through and through, he turns to his wife and says:

> 「The warmth of basking in the sun — no one knows of it. If I present it to my lord, he will surely reward me richly.」

Such was the worth he set on the simple warmth of sunlight that he thought it fit to offer it up to his lord for a handsome prize. The practice itself is plain: after ten in the morning, or in the afternoon, sit with your back to the sun for twenty minutes and let the warmth soak into your back.

Then add two or three small things: a stroll after lunch — a loop around the neighborhood will do; open the windows on fine days and let the rooms grow brighter; keep a steady routine and put the phone down at night. The *Suwen* says the winter mind should be "as if hidden, as if stored away" — to hold one's feelings in is not the same as shutting oneself in with them.

## V. Four Foods of the Season

**White radish (daikon).** "In winter eat radish, in summer eat ginger," the old saying goes. For one thing, winter eating is rich in tonics, and rich food easily stagnates into heat; radish moves food along and sends qi downward, the perfect companion for warming things like mutton and chestnuts. For another, the new radishes of this season are crisp and sweet. A pork-rib and radish soup asks the least of you; shredded and tossed with sesame oil, it is just as clean and refreshing.

**Chinese yam.** Wash the slender tiegun yam (the "iron-stick" variety of Chinese yam) skin and all, steam for twenty minutes, and eat it dipped in honey — it fortifies the spleen and fills the belly; or cut a few sections into millet porridge as it simmers, and one bowl warms you from head to toe.

**Chestnuts.** Stew them with chicken for soup, or simmer them with rice into chestnut congee. Sugar-roasted chestnuts smell wonderful, but seven or eight a day is enough — more and they'll bloat you.

**Black sesame.** The moment the cold sets in, hair goes dry and skin grows tight. Toast black sesame over a low fire, crush it, and stir a spoonful a day into porridge or milk — far better absorbed than chewing the seeds whole.
