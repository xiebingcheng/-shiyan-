> 「Lesser Cold is the node of the twelfth month. At the start of the month the cold is still small, hence the name; by mid-month it is great indeed.」 (「小寒，十二月节。月初寒尚小，故云。月半则大矣。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*, "Lesser Cold, the Twenty-Third"** (《月令七十二候集解·小寒第二十三》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. Small in Name, Not in Cold: When Heaven and Earth Freeze to the Utmost

Each year on 5–7 January of the Gregorian calendar, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 285°, **Lesser Cold** (*Xiaohan*, 小寒) arrives — the twenty-third of the twenty-four solar terms, the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. The name carries the word "lesser," so why are the coldest days of the year so often found here? Set the account against *shu jiu*, the folk custom of measuring the depth of winter in nine nine-day stretches counted from the winter solstice, and the ledger balances: the coldest of these stretches, the third nine, mostly falls within Lesser Cold, and the old saying gives up the diplomacy altogether — "Lesser Cold beats Greater Cold." The name defers; the real cold of heaven and earth does not.

Yet the three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — record, of all things, a stir of life:

- First pentad, "the wild geese turn north" (雁北乡). "Xiang means to lead the way": at this pentad of the second yang — the second stage of yang's slow climb back from its winter-solstice rebirth — the wild geese feel it first and swing their heads back north, "for birds take the season's qi ahead of all else";
- Second pentad, "the magpies begin to build nests" (鹊始巢). Magpies carry twigs in their beaks to raise their nests, and the nest openings "always face Tai Sui" — the direction of the year-star, whose seat shifts from year to year in Chinese folk reckoning;
- Third pentad, "the pheasants begin to call" (雉始雊). The pheasant's plumage is patterned and bright, hence it is styled "the bird of pattern and brightness, a yang bird"; at this season it opens its voice — "it sounds only after feeling the yang."

In a season when a splash of water freezes the moment it lands, the phenology — the old habit of reading the season by what beasts and plants do — is all motion: geese turning, magpies carrying twigs, pheasants finding their voices. The very depth of the cold is the moment when yang qi, the body's warm, active energy, quietly lifts its head. The people on the ground should take the hint: see the cold-proofing through in full, and let the mind not grey out along with the sky.

## II. Laba Porridge: Grains and Beans for the Base, Fruit and Nuts for the Finish

Once into the La month — the twelfth and last month of the lunar year — the first great matter of eating is **Laba porridge** (*labazhou*), for Laba, the eighth day of that month, mostly falls right around Lesser Cold. The most detailed account of what goes into this porridge was left by Fucha Dunchong of the Qing dynasty, in his *Yanjing Suishiji* (《燕京岁时记》, *Records of the Seasonal Customs of Yanjing*):

> 「Laba porridge is made with yellow millet, white rice, glutinous rice, millet, kernels of the water caltrop, chestnuts, red cowpeas, and peeled, pitted date paste, all boiled together in water. Beyond these, walnut kernels dyed red, apricot kernels, melon seeds, peanuts, hazelnut kernels, and pine nuts, together with white sugar, brown sugar, and suosuo grapes — tiny seedless grapes dried like raisins — are used to dot and tint the top.」

He adds one caution: "By no means put in lotus seeds, hyacinth beans, Job's tears, or longan — use them and they mar the flavor." The porridge aims at sweet stickiness and the whole household gathered round, not at a medicinal dish, and the more things go into the pot, the more the flavors run into one another. Unpacked, the list is a crew with clear divisions of labor: yellow millet, white rice, glutinous rice, and millet hold the base and answer for thickness and stickiness; the beans and the date paste answer for softness and sweetness; the walnuts, apricot kernels, melon seeds, and pine nuts, scattered on just before eating "to dot and tint," answer for fragrance and bite.

At home there is no need to take the list in full; an eight-character Chinese dictum carries it: "grains and beans for the base, dried fruit for the finish." A simple recipe for a family of four: 50 g of plain rice, 30 g of glutinous rice — the sticky, round-grain rice — 20 g of millet and 30 g of red beans, all soaked the night before; 6 red dates, split open and pitted, going into the pot with the beans; add about 1.2 liters of water, bring it to a boil over high heat, then turn down to a low simmer for 40 minutes before adding the three grains; keep cooking until the "rice oil" — the thin cream that forms on well-cooked congee — floats up and the smell of dates fills the house, a little over an hour in all. Raisins go in just before the pot comes off the fire, and the nuts, toasted till fragrant, are scattered on top as it is served. Stir the porridge sparingly — once the bottom scorches, all the work is undone.

Families in the north also see to one small thing on this day: Laba garlic — garlic steeped in vinegar until the cloves turn green through. Purple-skinned garlic is peeled clean and soaked in rice vinegar; the jar is sealed and set somewhere cool, and the cloves green a little more each day, ready to be opened just as the New Year arrives. The nursery rhyme sings: "Children, children, do not long for treats — once Laba is past, the New Year is on its way." From this bowl of porridge on, the year's business lines itself up on the calendar, one item after another.

## III. Holding Off Illness in the Third Nine: The Work Is in the Details

The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Great Treatise on the Mutual Correspondence of Yin and Yang" (阴阳应象大论), carries an old line: "Whoever is harmed by cold in winter will surely fall ill with a warmth-disease come spring" — the febrile illness Chinese medicine counts to spring's rising warmth. The cold you take in winter must be paid back come spring. That two-thousand-year ledger still balances today: in the third nine, respiratory illnesses and heart and stroke emergencies peak, and the trigger is usually some moment of getting chilled that no one thought worth minding.

First, going out. Do not set out for morning exercise too early: before sunrise the ground is at its coldest and blood pressure at its peak; if you want to move, wait for the sun to clear the horizon and the frost to melt, and stop once the body has warmed and sweats a little. Gloves, scarf, and earmuffs — feel for the three of them by the door before you leave; do not trust to memory.

Then the shifts between indoors and out. To plunge from a heated room straight into the icy wind makes the blood vessels clamp down abruptly, most dangerous of all for the old — before heading out, stand in the stairwell a minute or two and let the body take the temperature gap first. In the bath, do not chase the heat: the colder the day, the more one wants the water hotter and the soak longer, yet a rush of scalding water flings the vessels of the skin wide open, and stepping out afterward one catches cold all the more easily — the ones who grow dizzy in the bathroom are mostly the old. Sun Simiao's *Beiji Qianjin Yaofang* (《备急千金要方》, *Essential Prescriptions Worth a Thousand Pieces of Gold*, Tang dynasty), in its "Preface on Life-Cultivation," sets down a maxim of ten Chinese characters: "In winter do not go to the utmost of warmth; in summer do not exhaust coolness." Keep the water at 38 or 39 °C, finish the bath in ten minutes or so, and before getting out, warm the bathroom through; dry off and dress before you step out of it.

And a few odds and ends: brush and rinse with warm water in the morning; tuck a hot-water bottle under the covers before bed — steadier than an electric blanket left powered all night. Small things, but in the third nine not one of them may be let slide for a single day — cold is what accumulates, and so is illness.

## IV. Unhurried Before the New Year Shopping List

Once Lesser Cold is past, the New Year can no longer be held back. The bustle is unavoidable; the pace is yours to set: buy the New Year goods in two or three trips instead of hoarding it all for a market-rush at the year's very end; give the big chores — the great house-cleaning, the curing of meats — one a day, and do not cram them into the same weekend. Stick a note on the refrigerator door and strike through one item as it gets done — the most nourishing state of mind before the New Year is turning a snarl of tangles into a list.

One more small thing: change the lamp in the hallway or the study for a brighter one. Winter dark comes early, and in the instant you step in from work, what lights up is not the lamp but the solid feel of "I'm home." The New Year is not a thing to be raced: with work in your hands and a list in your mind, simply walk closer to it, a day at a time.

## V. Four Foods of the Season

- **Walnuts**: two or three a day as a snack, or cracked into hot porridge before bed; if the shells are too much trouble, buy the kernels, and stir them into milk and oatmeal in the morning;
- **Hawthorn**: the festival days are drawing near, and the table is getting richer. Pit 300 g of hawthorn berries and halve them; add 100 g of rock sugar and half a bowl of water; simmer over a low fire until the syrup turns glossy red and thick; leave to cool and jar it — old Beijing calls it *chao hongguo*, "stir-fried red fruit"; a spoonful after a meal cuts the grease; those whose stomachs easily turn sour should go easy on it, especially on an empty stomach;
- **Potherb mustard (xuelihong)**: the leading green of the La month's pickling. Wash it and let it wilt dry, then pack it into the crock in layers — a layer of greens, a layer of salt — pressed down firm; allow about half a jin of salt to every ten jin of greens (a jin being 500 g). let it pickle for half a month before opening the crock; chop and stir-fry it with soybeans, and its salty fragrance will hold its own against the rich meats of the first lunar month;
- **Lotus root**: winter lotus root is just in season for the soup pot. Blanch 500 g of pork ribs clean of blood-scum; take two sections of lotus root, scrape off the skin, and roll-cut them into thick, irregular chunks; add slices of ginger and plenty of hot water, and simmer over a low fire for an hour and a half, until the broth turns milky white and the lotus root goes soft and floury without breaking apart; pick the starchy kind — yellow-brown of skin, heavy in the hand — for the pot; the crisp kind is best simply stir-fried.
