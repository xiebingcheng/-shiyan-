> 「Greater Cold is the middle node: held up against Lesser Cold before it, it earns the word "great"... The cold qi has turned to its utmost extreme — hence it is called Greater Cold.」 (「大寒为中者，上形于小寒，故谓之大……寒气之逆极，故谓大寒。」)
>
> — **Cui Ling'en**, *Sanli Yizong* (《三礼义宗》, *Essential Meanings of the Three Rites*, Southern Liang dynasty), as quoted in the *Shoushi Tongkao* (《授时通考·天时》, *Comprehensive Treatise on the Seasons*, Qing dynasty)

## I. The Last Stop of the Twenty-Four Solar Terms

Each year on 19–21 January of the Gregorian calendar, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 300°, **Greater Cold** (*Dahan*, 大寒) arrives — the twenty-fourth and last of the twenty-four solar terms, the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. The *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* files it under "the middle node of the twelfth month," pressed right up against the gate of the year's end — folk say "once Greater Cold is past, a new year comes round again," and when this one is done, the first lunar month is standing at the door.

Read in sequence, the three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — read like a page torn from a countdown. First pentad, "the hens begin to brood" (鸡始乳): "The chicken is a creature of the wood phase — one of the five elements of Chinese thought; receiving the yang qi, it breeds and broods its eggs — hence 'brood,'" and the hen is the first to sense the newborn yang stirring, settling in to hatch her chicks; second pentad, "birds of prey turn fierce and swift" (征鸟厉疾): "'Zheng' means to strike; the striking, killing birds are the hawks and falcons, and by now they are at their fiercest and swiftest" — in the coldest days of the year the hawks hunt hardest; third pentad, "the ice on waters and marshes freezes to its belly" (水泽腹坚): "When ice first congeals it skins the surface alone; by this point it is frozen through, top to bottom — hence 'belly,' for the belly is what lies within" — the days when the freeze cuts deepest are exactly the countdown to spring. (All three glosses are quoted from the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*.)

## II. The Kitchen Draws Its Close: Glutinous Rice and Eight-Treasure Rice

On what to eat on the day of Greater Cold, Guangdong has its own answer: glutinous rice — the sticky, round-grain variety that southern kitchens steam and fry. The local custom is a bowl of hot glutinous rice on Dahan, in pursuit of a feeling the Cantonese call *nyun lap lap* (暖笠笠), their dialect's way of saying warm and cozy through and through. The properly done version is the raw-fried glutinous rice: soak the rice two to three hours ahead, drain it, and steam it through; dice Chinese cured sausage and cured pork belly — the salt-cured meats of winter, known as *la wei* — into small bits; soak dried shiitake mushrooms and small dried shrimp until soft, then chop them; heat the wok, sauté minced ginger till fragrant, fry the cured meats first to press out their oil, return the rice to the wok and toss it loose, splash in a little light soy sauce, and to finish, off the heat, scatter scallion greens and a handful of fried peanuts. On a home stove an easier way round is to steam the rice until just shy of done first and fry it after — just as fragrant. Count on about 100 to 150 g of dry glutinous rice per person for a meal; eat it hot, and chew slowly.

Yet the *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, the *Compendium of Materia Medica*), in its Grain Section under the heading "rice," leaves glutinous rice one caution: "Glutinous rice is sticky and binding by nature, hard to digest; children and those who are ill should above all avoid it." The words fit Greater Cold especially well — a whole winter of rich herbal pastes, healing dishes, and thick soups has been banked up to here, and it is time to taper off. Tapering off does not mean going vegetarian overnight; it means easing the "richness" down bit by bit: milky-white broths give way to clear ones, a plain congee with a simple side joins the table, and the glutinous rice steps back from staple to treat. Let the spleen and stomach — the pair Chinese medicine charges with digestion — travel light to meet the Beginning of Spring (*Lichun*).

The festival table also makes room for Eight-Treasure Rice (*babaofan*): steam the glutinous rice and toss it with sugar and oil, line the bottom of the bowl with red dates, lotus seeds, and sweetened red-bean paste, steam it through once more, and turn it out upside down into one whole, unbroken round — in Jiangnan, the country south of the lower Yangtze, it is often the dish that closes the New Year's Eve dinner. The "eight treasures" are a lucky charm: treasure flowing in from all eight directions, the family round and reunited. Made at home, steam one bowl and share it — a spoonful each, a taste of sweetness, and that is enough.

## III. Put Winter's Storing to Rights, and Make Room for Spring

Keeping the cold at bay is still the first order of business. The fiercest cold snaps of the year tend to land right around Greater Cold. Head, neck, waist, and legs stay wrapped as ever; the old should wait for the sun to clear the horizon before going out for morning exercise; and among the New Year goods, lay in an extra knob of ginger.

Yet the daily rhythm of Greater Cold wants a little "moving" alongside all this "keeping" — the earliest drift from winter's storing toward spring's arising. The routine can start with a small adjustment: rise ten or twenty minutes earlier than in deep winter, cut the lie-in by half, and sleep is still ample — only there is no call to sleep until the sun stands three poles high. On clear days, invite the bedding out into the sun: the north is dry-cold with many clear skies, so two or three hours around noon will do, and before bringing the quilt in, give it a light pat and a shake; the south is damp-cold, so watch for your window of fair weather, and if no sun can be had, dry the bedding with heat instead — do not let the quilts carry damp into the New Year.

Then comes the great year-end sweep. Old Suzhou custom called it "striking the dust," and Gu Lu of the Qing sets it down plainly in Scroll 12 of the *Qingjia Lu* (《清嘉录》, *Records of the Festive Seasons of the Qing*): "As the La month — the twelfth and last month of the lunar year — wears toward its end, choose from the almanac a day marked fit for sweeping the house, and clear the dust and grime from courtyard and rooms. Some keep to the twenty-third, some the twenty-fourth, some the twenty-seventh; folk call it striking the dust." In the north the saying runs simply: "On the twenty-fourth, sweep the house." It is New Year custom, and it is heavy work too: tackle it zone by zone, day by day — do not count on wiping the whole house clean in one go; for high windows and top-of-the-cabinet dusting the stool must be steady, and what you cannot reach, hand to someone who can — the old should not climb; bend the knees before moving anything; give it thirty or forty minutes, then rest; and if you have sweated through, wait indoors until the sweat has settled before you face the cold wind.

The windows must be aired too: twice a day, fifteen minutes to half an hour each time, chosen from between ten in the morning and three in the afternoon, steering clear of the coldest hours at early morning and dusk; before bed, give the room one thorough airing and then shut the window tight — better than leaving a slit open all night long.

## IV. Taking Stock and Letting Go at Year's End

The *Jingchu Suishiji* (《荆楚岁时记》, *Record of the Seasons and Customs of Jingchu*), compiled by Zong Lin of the Southern Liang dynasty, records the year's end: "At year's end, every household lays out dishes of meat and vegetable and gathers at the place where the rice of the passing year is kept, to welcome the new year; all come together and drink deep." The rice kept over into the new year — the *susui fan*, the "rice of the year that is ending" — "is then cast out into the streets and lanes, by way of casting off the old and taking in the new." Casting off the old and taking in the new — and when did that ever speak of rice alone?

The year-end work on the mind, to say it plainly, is taking stock and letting go — and it can be done as three small concrete things. First, keep the year's ledger: not an audit for missing cash, but a look at where the days actually went — bills, photo albums, chat histories all count — and see what the days were actually spent on. Second, write the coming year on a single sheet of paper, three things and no more, and when it is written, stick it to the refrigerator door. Making good on three things in a year is already fine going; the fewer you write, the better you remember. Third, bring the house into order: start with the corner you most want changed next year — the drawer jammed with clutter, the balcony corner stacked with cardboard boxes. When the rooms run smooth, the creases in the mind flatten out a notch as well.

## V. Three Things in the New Year Fruit Box

Peanuts — known in folk speech as the "longevity fruit" — are safest boiled in the shell: simmer a potful, shell and all, with salt and two slices of ginger, then let them air until half-dry, and eat a dozen or so a day; the salt-baked kind leans dry and warming, and those whose throats already feel parched should keep their distance. Red melon seeds or sunflower seeds: cracking seeds is not about getting full, it is about slowing down — one seed at a time, keeping company in conversation; a small handful, about 20 g, is the limit, and beyond that the mouth goes dry and the teeth pay for it. Dried persimmon — frosted white outside, soft and sweet within — carries a lucky turn of speech: the fruit's name, *shi* (柿), sounds exactly like the word for "affairs" (事), so the box wishes everyone "may every affair go as you wish" (事事如意). Half a fruit a day; steamed soft, it goes down more easily, and a pot of hot hawthorn water alongside — the tart red berry Chinese kitchens simmer as a digestive — is just the thing to work off the sweetness.

And alongside the fruit box — the box of treats a household sets out for New Year callers — keep one cool, moistening plate to anchor the spread: water chestnuts (*ma ti*) are in season right now; peel them and simmer them in water — sweet and clean, they cut the dryness of all the roasted seeds. They grow in water, so cooking them through is the safe way to eat them. The fruit box is the rich; this plate is the plain — the eating of Greater Cold, in the end, comes down to this one mouthful of balance.
