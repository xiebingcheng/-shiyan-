> 「Beginning of Spring is the node of the first month. 'Li' means to establish, to begin. Of the qi of the five elements, what departs passes on and what arrives takes up the thread, and they meet here; the qi of spring wood now first arrives, and so it is called 'establishing.'」 (「立春，正月节。立，建始也。五行之气往者过来者续于此。而春木之气始至，故谓之立也。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*, "Beginning of Spring, the First"** (《月令七十二候集解·立春第一》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. What the Term Means: Where Winter's Storing Comes to an End

**Beginning of Spring** (*Lichun*, 立春) stands first among the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. Each year, on a date between 3 and 5 February of the Gregorian calendar, the sun reaches celestial longitude 315° and the term begins. The count of the terms starts with it, and the single word "li" tells what that means: what departs passes on, what arrives takes up the thread — winter ends here, and spring begins.

The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — are all about this one word "gradually":

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The east wind unfreezes the ice** (东风解冻) | 「What froze through the winter dissolves when it meets the spring wind.」 |
| **Second** | **Hibernating insects begin to stir** (蛰虫始振) | 「The insects hidden away through the cold wake and stir, one and all, as the qi arrives.」 |
| **Third** | **The fish rise to bear the ice** (鱼陟负冰) | 「In the depth of cold the fish lie low at the bottom of the water; when the yang qi of the first month arrives, they swim up and draw near the ice.」 |

The insects only "begin to stir" — moving, but not yet out of their hiding places; the fish, too, only swim up beneath the ice. The phenology is like this, and so is the weather: Beginning of Spring is only the messenger, and the cold does not quit the stage the moment the news is delivered. Take this in first, and the talk of what to eat and how to dress that follows will not go astray.

## II. Eating Customs and Nourishment: A Bite of Spring's Radish

In the old days, the officials had one piece of business to see to before anything else on this day: *da chun*, "beating the spring" — the rite of striking a model ox, the spring ox, to open the farming year. Fucha Dunchong of the Qing records it in his *Yanjing Suishiji* (《燕京岁时记》, *Records of the Seasonal Customs of Yanjing*), under the entry "Beating the Spring":

> 「The Ministry of Rites presented the spring-mountain throne seat — a throne stand carved with spring-landscape motifs, an allegory of the season; the Prefecture of the Capital presented the picture of the spring ox. When the rites were done and the officials had returned to their offices, they led out the spring ox and struck it — this was called 'beating the spring.'」 (「礼部呈进春山宝座，顺天府呈进春牛图，礼毕回署，引春牛而击之，曰打春。」)
>
> — **Fucha Dunchong**, ***Yanjing Suishiji*, "Beating the Spring"** (《燕京岁时记·打春》, *Records of the Seasonal Customs of Yanjing*, Qing dynasty)

The rite of whipping the spring ox was meant to urge every farming household to ready its fields; one lash fell, and the notice was out to all the villages around.

When the notice reached the dinner table, it took the shape of **biting the spring** (*yaochun*) — the custom of eating radish as the year opens. The same entry puts it more plainly:

> 「On this day, the wealthy families mostly ate spring pancakes, while the women bought radishes and ate them — 'biting the spring,' they called it, for it was said to drive off spring drowsiness.」 (「是日，富家多食春饼，妇女等多买萝卜而食之，曰咬春，谓可以却春困也。」)

Why the radish, of all things? A winter of stewed meats and rich flavors has left the stomach and bowels at their most clogged, and the radish — acrid with a touch of coolness — moves the qi and cuts stagnation, just the one ingredient a change of season calls for. There is no ceremony required in eating it; three ways, all worth taking.

Biting it raw is the most satisfying. Pick a crisp young radish or a *xinlimei* — the Beijing variety that hides a rose-red heart — wash and slice it, and take a few bites first thing in the morning: a sharp, peppery crackle, and a lucky phrase to go with it: bite down, and spring is yours to hold.

Dressed cold is the easiest. Cut the radish into fine shreds, salt them lightly to draw out the water, squeeze them dry, then toss with black vinegar, a pinch of sugar, and a few drops of sesame oil. A little dish before the meal — it cuts grease and whets the appetite.

Rolled in a pancake is the merriest of the three — and here north and south part ways. In the north they griddle **spring pancakes** (*chunbing*, thin sheets of wheat-flour dough) and roll them around the mixed stir-fry — bean sprouts, Chinese chives, glass noodles, egg, and shredded pork fried together; wrap the lot in the thin sheet, and eat it from one end to the other. In Jiangnan, the country south of the lower Yangtze, people more often fry spring rolls, and the filling there, too, never does without bean sprouts and chives. Sprout and chive alike stand for rising, out-thrusting growth; the year's plan begins — quite literally — with this bit of new green at the tip of the chopsticks.

## III. Daily Life: What "Spring Covering" Really Covers Is the Pace

The folk proverb says, "Cover up in spring and brave the frost in autumn, and no stray illness will find you." That "covering" — **spring covering** (*chun wu*), the old rule of keeping the winter layers on well past the calendar's turn to spring — still applies after Beginning of Spring, and not because the old saying is stubborn. It answers a season that cannot make up its mind: the day's sun warms you into a sweat, a wind in the early morning or evening undoes it, and the cold snap that doubles back once spring has begun arrives whenever it pleases.

Qiu Chuji of the Yuan dynasty, in the spring chapter of his *Treatise on Life-Cultivation According to the Seasons* (《摄生消息论》), goes into the matter in fine detail:

> 「The weather veers between cold and warm; the padded clothes must not be cast off all at once. The old, whose qi is weak and whose bones are loose and bodies frail, let wind and cold slip past the skin's defenses all too easily. Keep lined garments at hand, change into them when it warms, shed one layer at a time — never strip them off in a single go.」 (「天气寒暄不一，不可顿去棉衣，老人气弱，骨疏体怯，风冷易伤腠理，时备夹衣，遇暖易之，一重渐减一重，不可暴去。」)
>
> — **Qiu Chuji**, ***Treatise on Life-Cultivation According to the Seasons*, "Messages of Life-Cultivation for Spring"** (《摄生消息论·春季摄生消息》, Yuan dynasty)

The key is the phrase "**shed one layer at a time**" — the padded clothes come off piece by piece; clearing the wardrobe in one sweep is not permitted. The same book quotes a proverb on "clothes added and shed by the hour": adjust up and down through the day as it warms and cools — a far sounder rule than picking one fixed date to retire the long johns. The same chapter carries a little formula besides: "When the body feels quite hot, ease off an upper garment; when it feels a slight chill, do not force yourself to bear it — put clothing on at once." Feel hot? Shed one piece. Feel cold? Do not tough it out. The whole rhythm of covering and shedding lives in these two sentences.

And where should the covering go? "**Thick below, light above**." Cao Tingdong of the Qing, in his *Lao Lao Heng Yan* (《老老恒言》, *Everyday Words for the Aged*), puts it without ceremony:

> 「The spring ice has not yet melted; better the lower body be too warm than not warm enough, while the upper body may be lightly reduced — this is how the life-giving qi of yang is nourished.」 (「春冰未泮，下体宁过于暖，上体无妨略减，所以养阳之生气。」)
>
> — **Cao Tingdong**, ***Lao Lao Heng Yan*, "At Ease at Home"** (《老老恒言·燕居》, Qing dynasty)

Lower back, knees, and feet should err on the side of thickness; the upper body may shed a little — the point is to keep the body's yang qi, its warm, active energy, banked low where it takes root. And the *Treatise on Life-Cultivation According to the Seasons* adds one pressing reminder: "Do not let the back turn cold; cold injures the lung, and brings a blocked nose and coughing." So before you peel off the outer coat, lay a hand on your own back first and see that it is still warm.

For the daily schedule, the *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》), gives the standard answer for Beginning of Spring:

> 「The three months of spring are what is called *fa chen* — the season when all that was stored away puts forth and is renewed. Heaven and Earth together give life, and the ten thousand things flourish. Lie down at night and rise early; walk at ease in the courtyard. Let the hair hang loose and the body relax, and so let the will come to life. Give life and do not kill; give and do not take away; reward and do not punish. This is the answer to the qi of spring and the way of nourishing life; to go against it injures the liver, and in summer brings illness of the cold kind, leaving too little in store for summer's growth.」 (「春三月，此谓发陈。天地俱生，万物以荣，夜卧早起，广步于庭，被发缓形，以使志生，生而勿杀，予而勿夺，赏而勿罚，此春气之应，养生之道也；逆之则伤肝，夏为寒变，奉长者少。」)

"**Lie down at night and rise early**" is not an invitation to stay up late; it means setting back the clock of winter's "lie down early, rise late" by a notch — to bed a little later than in deep winter, up a little earlier than in deep winter. The way to put it on the ground is plain. On waking, open the windows first and let the air circulate. Then use the fingers as a comb, stroking from the forehead to the back of the head for two minutes — the qi that winter has held bottled up for a whole season is let upward with those few dozen strokes. Then go downstairs and walk at an easy pace for a quarter of an hour, steps unhurried, no need to work up a sweat: that is "walking at ease in the courtyard."

## IV. Emotions: Write "the Year's Plan" a Little Smaller

The proverb says it once more: "the year's plan is made in spring." People hear that as pressure. What the *Suwen*'s "so let the will come to life" actually asks for is a will that unfolds, not a grand resolution. "Give life and do not kill; give and do not take away; reward and do not punish" — go easier on others, and go easier on yourself.

For putting it into practice, try three small things. Write only the one most important item of the plan, stick it on the refrigerator door, and do not be greedy for more. Plant a pot of garlic sprouts or scallions on the balcony; a few days of watering and you can watch it shoot up — more bracing than any amount of sermonizing. Then choose one corner and clear it out — a drawer, a desk, anything; the patch of empty space you make is this year's fresh opening.

## V. Foods of the Season

**Radish** — good raw or dressed cold as above; a few slices dropped into the stew pot also cut through the heavy richness of winter's fattening.

**Chinese chives** — spring chives are the most tender; they go into the mixed stir-fry or into *jiucai hezi*, the pan-fried chive pockets; their acrid warmth helps spring's rising, and whatever is left overnight, you pass on.

**Bean sprouts** — sprout your own mung beans, pinch off the root-ends and tail-ends, and stir-fry them plain: crisp and juicy; add a handful of chives and that is the taste of Beginning of Spring.

**Shepherd's purse** — already pushing up at the field edges; blanch it and toss with dried tofu, or wrap a potful of shepherd's-purse wontons — the first fresh bite of the opening year.
