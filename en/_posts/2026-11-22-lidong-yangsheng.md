> 「Beginning of Winter is the node of the tenth month. For the word 'li' see the explanation given before. Winter means the end — all things are gathered in and stored away.」 (「立冬，十月节。立字解见前。冬，终也，万物收藏也。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*, "Beginning of Winter, the Nineteenth"** (《月令七十二候集解·立冬第十九》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. What the Term Means: Winter Means the End

**Beginning of Winter** (*Lidong*, 立冬) is the nineteenth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — and the first of winter. Each year on 7 or 8 November of the Gregorian calendar, the sun reaches celestial longitude 225° and the term begins; the lunar calendar sets it in the tenth month, and so the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* calls it 「the node of the tenth month」 (「十月节」).

「For the word 'li' see the explanation given before」 (「立字解见前」) — the meaning of "li" was set out under the Beginning of Spring entry: 「'Li' means to establish, to begin」 (「立，建始也」). The pair of "li" terms, Beginning of Spring and Beginning of Winter, stands at the two ends of the year: one end establishes and begins, the other declares the end. **Winter means the end — all things are gathered in and stored away**: the crops go into the granary, the hibernating insects seal their burrows, and heaven and earth sit down to close the year's books.

All three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — carry the word "begins":

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **Water begins to form ice** (水始冰) | 「The surface of the water just begins to congeal, not yet to hardness.」 (「水面初凝，未至于坚也。」) The first thin ice is forming |
| **Second** | **The ground begins to freeze** (地始冻) | 「The qi of the soil congeals with cold, not yet to splitting.」 (「土气凝寒，未至于坼。」) The ground freezes on top, but does not yet split |
| **Third** | **The pheasants enter the great waters and become clams** (雉入大水为蜃) | 「The *zhi* is the wild pheasant」 (「雉，野鸡」), and the *shen* is a great clam. The pheasants vanish without a trace, and the great clams along the shore bear stripes and colors like a pheasant's plumage — the ancients said the birds had turned into them |

The *Book of Rites*, in its "Monthly Ordinances" chapter (《礼记·月令》), records the first month of winter:

> 「On the day of Beginning of Winter, the Son of Heaven in person led the three dukes, the nine ministers, and the grand officers to welcome winter at the northern outskirts; on their return, he rewarded the families of those who had died in the state's service and gave relief to the orphaned and the widowed.」 (「立冬之日，天子亲帅三公、九卿、大夫以迎冬于北郊，还反，赏死事，恤孤寡。」)

The same chapter carries one more phrase: 「**the Son of Heaven begins to wear furs**」 (「天子始裘」) — the court changed into furs on this very day. The common people could not match that pomp, so they carried the day into the rice bowl instead. The Minnan (southern Fujian) saying goes, "**Tonify at Beginning of Winter, and fill an empty mouth**." In the north they fold dumplings — *jiaozi* — for the turn from autumn to winter is precisely the 「jiaozi moment」, the meeting-point of one time and the next, and the dumpling's own name sounds just like the word for it. Nanjing's old saying runs, "Half a scallion a day, and your legs will carry no wind ailment into winter," and on this day the city chews raw scallions. In the south, chicken, duck, fish, and mutton all come to the table together, and Fujian and Taiwan add a pot of sesame-oil chicken or ginger duck.

Eating is one half of the day; the other half is storing. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Six Sections and the Visceral Manifestations" (《素问·六节藏象论》), says:

> 「The kidney governs hibernation — it is the root of sealing and storing, the dwelling place of the essence... and it communicates with the qi of winter.」 (「肾者，主蛰，封藏之本，精之处也……通于冬气。」)

The qi of winter communicates with the kidney, and the kidney presides over sealing and storing — to nourish what is stored is the master rule of the three months that run from Beginning of Winter.

## II. Winter Tonification: A Bowl of Clear-Simmered Mutton Soup, a Pot of Chinese Cabbage Stewed with Glass Noodles

Winter tonification is not fierce tonification. Its virtue lies in frequency and small amounts, filling the year's deficits warmly, a little at a time.

**Clear-simmered mutton soup** (for three): mutton, 600 g (foreleg or rib cuts), cut into large chunks and started in a pot of cold water with 15 ml of cooking wine and two slices of ginger; once the scum has floated and been skimmed, lift the meat out and rinse it clean. In a clay pot, pour in plenty of hot water, about 1500 ml, and add the mutton, two sections of scallion whites, and three slices of ginger; simmer seventy minutes over a low fire. Just before it leaves the heat, stir in 4 g of salt and 1–2 g of freshly ground white pepper, and switch off the fire and scatter cilantro over it. The white pepper must wait for the end: all its warmth lives in the acrid fragrance, and a long boil scatters it.

The cautions first: those running a fever, with a swollen and painful throat, or constipated with dark urine should not touch it; for those of yin-deficiency constitution — heat in the palms and soles, a red tongue with scant coating — halve the white pepper; and those with uric acid running high should stop at a taste of the broth.

Beyond the broth, the winter table cannot do without Chinese cabbage. In the *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*) the Chinese cabbage is recorded under the name *song*, chosen for its sound-alike with the word for pine, and the entry's explanation of the name quotes Lu Dian's *Pinya* (《埤雅》):

> 「The song holds out into winter and withers late, is in leaf through all four seasons, and has the constancy of the pine.」 (「菘性凌冬晚凋，四时常见，有松之操。」)

Shizhen further records that it 「**frees the stomach and bowel and clears the vexation in the chest**」 (「通利肠胃，除胸中烦」) — through a winter of stewed meats and heavy flavors, it is just the broom the stomach and bowel want.

**Chinese cabbage stewed with glass noodles** (for two or three): Chinese cabbage, 500 g — ribs and leaves separated, the thick ribs sliced thin on the bias; glass noodles (*fentiao*), 100 g, soaked soft in warm water and snipped twice with the scissors; pork belly, 60 g, sliced thin. Set the pork belly into a cold wok and render its fat over a medium-low fire; add the scallion and ginger and fry until fragrant. In go the cabbage ribs first, two minutes over a high fire, then the leaves, tossed until they wilt; pour 10 ml of light soy sauce down the side of the wok, then add about 400 ml of hot water. Lay the glass noodles over the cabbage — never against the bottom of the pot — cover, and simmer eight to ten minutes over a medium-low fire, until the noodles have gone translucent and only a shallow layer of broth remains; add 2 g of salt and take it off the fire. Do not cook the broth down dry: the point of the dish in winter is the little thickened broth the noodles hold.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: Cold Hands and Feet, and a Cold of Wind and Cold at Its Start

Cold hands and feet are the commonest complaint of the two weeks before the heating comes on, and the cause is mostly sitting still for too long — the yang qi, the body's warm, active energy, never reaches the tips. Once an hour in the chair, get up and spend a minute rising onto the tiptoes; stick heat packs on the lower back and belly or on the back itself, never on the soles of the feet — doze off that way and a low-temperature burn comes easily; and before sleep, soak the feet in warm water, stopping once the tops of the feet flush red.

One red line: cold in the legs and feet on **one side only**, calves that ache and swell after a stretch of walking and demand a rest, toes turned dark in color — this is not mere aversion to the cold; it looks like a matter of the blood vessels of the lower limbs. Go to the hospital. Those with diabetes have dulled feeling in their feet; test the water for the foot-soak with an elbow first.

Colds of wind and cold, too, grow more frequent in these weeks. The signals at the start: aversion to cold strong and fever light, a nose running with clear water, the back of the neck drawn tight. Two things above all to shun: reaching first for cooling herbal teas — cold laid upon cold — and bundling up to sweat it out by force. For the measure of sweating, Zhang Zhongjing set the standard in the directions for taking Cinnamon Twig Decoction (*guizhi tang*) in the *Shanghan Lun* (《伤寒论》, *Treatise on Cold Damage*):

> 「Sip a little over a *sheng* of hot thin gruel, to help the strength of the medicine」 (「啜热稀粥一升余，以助药力」) 「a sweat that comes out over the whole body, faint and slight, is best... and if it does not, the illness will surely not be dispelled.」 (「遍身漐漐，微似有汗者益佳……病必不除。」)

Even the medicine was to be washed down with a bowl of hot gruel — a *sheng* or more of it, the *sheng* being a Han-dynasty measure of capacity — and the sweat was to come out damp and mild: *zhizhi*, a light sweat that leaves the skin moist. A household without the formula may borrow the method all the same: when a cold of wind and cold first stirs, eat a bowl of hot congee while it is hot, sleep wrapped in the quilt, and push the quilt back once the body feels damp. High fever that will not come down, a throat gone to pus, chest tightness with shortness of breath — go to the hospital.

## IV. Daily Life: Follow the Sun

The *Suwen*'s "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》) sets the master rule for the three months of winter; from Beginning of Winter on, carry it out as written:

> 「The three months of winter are called closing and storing: water freezes and the earth cracks; do not disturb the yang. Retire early and rise late — wait for the sunlight... do not let the skin leak and sweat, or the qi will be stripped away in haste. This is how to answer the qi of winter: the way of nourishing what is stored. To go against it injures the kidneys, and in spring brings wei and jue — flaccidity and reversal cold of the limbs — leaving too little in store for spring's birth.」 (「冬三月，此谓闭藏，水冰地坼，无扰乎阳，早卧晚起，必待日光……无泄皮肤，使气亟夺，此冬气之应，养藏之道也。逆之则伤肾，春为痿厥，奉生者少。」)

**Retire early and rise late — wait for the sunlight** (「早卧晚起，必待日光」): into bed half an hour earlier than in autumn; up when the sky has lightened, never ahead of the sun. Next spring's strength is slept into store through the winter.

Exercise moves its hour with the season: before sunrise the ground is at its coldest and the pavement at its slickest, so the pre-dawn workout moves to after ten in the morning. Warm the body and stop at a light sweat — 「do not let the skin leak」 (「无泄皮肤」) is the measure; once the sweat runs, the yang qi leaks away with it. And after eight or nine at night, no more vigorous exercise.

The old saying goes, "One day into Beginning of Winter, the water runs three-tenths colder." Autumn freezing (*qiu dong*, the autumn-side counterpart of spring covering) drew its endpoint at Frost's Descent, and Beginning of Winter is no time to freeze on past it: the thin coat gives way to the thick one, a scarf guards the nape of the neck, and the ankles tuck into thick socks. The two weeks before the heating comes on are the hardest of all, wind and cold outside the window and dryness inside it — mop the floors damp or hang out a wet towel before sleep; open the windows ten minutes in the afternoon to change the air; preheat an electric blanket before bed and switch it off the moment you get in.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Beef**. Winter tonification does not run by the mutton road alone. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Beasts" section (兽部), records beef as 「**settling the center and boosting the qi, and nourishing the spleen and stomach**」 (「安中益气，养脾胃」). In the pot: 500 g of beef brisket, blanched clean, then an hour and a half of slow simmering over a low fire, with potato chunks in for the last twenty minutes; for a weak appetite, the stewed-through meat sits far easier on the stomach than any quick stir-fry.

**Winter jujubes**. Around Beginning of Winter the winter jujube's season is at its tail — eat them fresh while they last. The fresh jujube ranks among the top few of the common fruits for vitamin C, and of that hardly anything survives the drying into red dates. Five or six a day is the measure; the skin is tough, and those with a weak stomach should chew slowly and well.

**Goji berries**. Winter answers to the kidney, and goji nourishes the liver and the kidney. A small pinch a day, about 10 g — chewed straight, or stirred into congee. Set them aside while a cold with fever, or a bout of loose bowels, runs its course.
