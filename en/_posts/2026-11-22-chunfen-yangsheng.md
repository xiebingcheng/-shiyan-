> 「Of the essentials of yin and yang: when the yang is kept close and secure, the body holds firm... when yin lies calm and yang holds secure, the essence and spirit are in good order; when yin and yang part and sever, the essence and qi come to an end.」 (「凡阴阳之要，阳密乃固……阴平阳秘，精神乃治；阴阳离决，精气乃绝。」)
>
> — ***Huangdi Neijing · Suwen · "Treatise on the Vital Qi That Communicates with Heaven"*** (《黄帝内经·素问·生气通天论》)

## I. What the Term Means: The Most "Even" Day of the Year

**The Spring Equinox** (*Chunfen*, 春分) is the day the sun reaches celestial longitude 0° and its light strikes the equator straight on, so that day and night stand equal the world over. Of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — most take their names as footnotes to farm work or to what plants and beasts are doing; the Spring Equinox alone is named by the sky itself, for the simple fact that day and night, neither more nor less, split exactly in half.

Wu Cheng of the Yuan dynasty puts it flatly in the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* (《月令七十二候集解》):

> 「The Spring Equinox is the middle node of the second month. 'Fen' means the half. This falls at the half of spring's ninety days, and so it is called the division.」 (「春分，二月中。分者，半也。此当九十日之半，故谓之分。」)

It is the half of day and night, and the half of spring's ninety days, both at once. The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — follow in this order:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The *xuan* bird arrives** (玄鸟至) | 「The *xuan* bird — the 'dark bird' — is the swallow. Gao You says: it comes at the Spring Equinox and goes at the Autumn Equinox.」 |
| **Second** | **Thunder first sounds** (雷乃发声) | 「Yin and yang pressing against each other beget thunder. By this time the four yang lines of the hexagram the old books assign to this month have swelled toward fullness, yet yin still remains, and as they grind together the sound breaks out.」 |
| **Third** | **Lightning first flashes** (始电) | 「Lightning is the light of yang. The four yang lines grow and flourish; meeting the hour when the qi vents itself, the light appears.」 |

The notion of the three pentads goes back at least to the *Yi Zhou Shu* (《逸周书》, a Zhou-era compendium of documents), in its "Explanations of the Seasonal Instructions" (《时训解》): 「On the day of the Spring Equinox the *xuan* bird arrives; five days after, thunder first sounds; five days after that, lightning first flashes.」 (「春分之日玄鸟至，又五日雷乃发声，又五日始电。」) The swallow — the *xuan* bird of the old texts — arrives on schedule, thunder and lightning take the stage one after another; two forces of heaven and earth cross paths and yet each keeps its place. Folk custom adds egg-standing to the day's amusements — balancing a raw egg upright on its end — with balance as the point; the proverb has it that "when the Spring Equinox comes, the eggs stand tall," and the luck being courted is precisely this word "even."

**Take balance as the aim** — that is the governing idea of this piece: eat nothing that leans too far cold or too far hot, keep no schedule that runs to extremes, and let no mood surge and sink unchecked.

## II. Nourishment: Level-Natured Foods Lead, Taste the Freshness Without Overdoing It

What should you eat at the Spring Equinox? Hold the list of dishes a moment; the answer is really one word: pairing. In a season when cold and heat are evenly matched, the table had better balance them too — a cooling vegetable goes with a level-natured soy food, a warming vegetable gets a cooling preparation, and that is steadier advice than chasing any single "great tonic." The three dishes below are all built on that idea.

**Shepherd's-purse and tofu thick soup**. Cut 300 g of soft tofu into small cubes; wash and chop 150 g of shepherd's purse, the delicate wild green with the cress flavor; finely chop two slices of ginger; set aside two spoonfuls of starch slurry. Bring about 800 ml of water to the boil, add the tofu first and cook it three minutes, then add the shepherd's purse and the ginger, stir in the slurry to thicken it lightly, season with a little salt, take the pot off the fire and finish with a few drops of sesame oil. The Beginning-of-Spring essay mentioned shepherd's-purse wontons in passing; here is another way with the same green. Shepherd's purse is slightly cool and tofu cool and moistening; cooked into a soup they turn mild together, and the two slices of ginger make it steadier still. A folk saying praises it — "on the third day of the third month, shepherd's purse outdoes the wonder drug" — and the same praise carries its warning, for this freshness has a season: once the plant flowers, it turns old and coarse. Those whose spleen and stomach — in Chinese-medicine terms, the digestive core — run cold and weak, with loose stools as the telltale, should have one bowl and stop, and not make a meal of it.

**Toon shoots with tofu**. You need 80 g of Chinese toon shoots — the young leaves of the toon tree, a flavor the Chinese kitchen waits on all year — one block of soft tofu, about 300 g, and a little salt and sesame oil. Blanch the tofu two minutes in boiling water, lift it out and dice it; then blanch the toon shoots about half a minute — the moment the leaves turn bright green, fish them out, chill them, and chop; toss the two together with salt and sesame oil, and it is done. The toon runs warming by nature; eaten alone it warms and scatters, and the cool, moistening tofu pulls it back into line. The ancients gave the warning early: the *Shiliaoben* (《食疗本草》, *Materia Medica of Dietary Therapy*; written by Meng Shen of the Tang and enlarged by Zhang Ding) says under the entry for the toon (椿) that it 「stirs the wind — in Chinese-medicine terms, provokes old wind-type ailments, chronic sores among them — steaming through the twelve channels — the conduits of qi and blood — and the five yin and six yang organs; eaten in excess, it clouds the mind and leaves the blood and qi faint」 (「动风，熏十二经脉、五脏六腑，多食令人神不清，血气微」). Today we know that toon shoots run comparatively high in nitrites, and blanching before cooking removes most of them — so the half minute in boiling water is not a step to skip. For anyone with chronic or long-standing sores, better still to stop at a taste.

**Malantou with dried tofu**. Take 200 g of malantou — the young leaves of a wild kin of the chrysanthemum — and 50 g of fragrant dried tofu (*xianggan*, the firm pressed tofu sold in bricks), with sesame oil, salt, and a pinch of sugar no bigger than a fingernail. Blanch the malantou about a minute, chill it, squeeze it dry, and chop; simmer the dried tofu two minutes, mince it fine, and toss the two together. In the Jiangsu–Zhejiang country of the lower Yangtze this is spring's most ordinary cold dish: malantou carries a hint of coolness, dried tofu is level in nature, and one toss brings them even; a little sesame oil lifts the wild-herb fragrance, and the sugar — a fingernail's worth, no more — draws out the savor without leaving sweetness behind.

Lingnan — the far southern country around Guangdong — keeps yet another custom: gathering "spring greens" at the Spring Equinox, a wild amaranth known locally as *chunbi hao*, and boiling them with fish slices into a rolling soup, to the old rhyme, "spring soup fills and rinses the organs clean, from liver to gut; a whole household, old and young, is safe and well." North and south eat differently and mean the same: taste the first of the season by all means — only do not chase it to the full. Stop at seven or eight tenths; that is the measure the Spring Equinox teaches.

## III. Daily Life: Set the Clock by Day and Night

A day split even between light and dark is the best day there is for resetting one's schedule — let activity run as long as the daylight runs, and wrap up the day's work as early as the dark falls. On the ground: in bed before eleven at night, up around six-thirty in the morning; no staying up late, and no sleeping in. Sleeping in is the one to watch in spring. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Elucidation of the Five Qi" (《素问·宣明五气》), says that 「prolonged lying injures the qi」 (「久卧伤气」) — oversleep, and you come away dull and feeble instead of rested. With the daylight lengthening, take twenty minutes of sun around ten in the morning and again at three or four in the afternoon; it outworks any pick-me-up drink.

Choose gentle exercise. The outing into the spring green goes without saying; flying a kite suits this season best of all — outdoors, unhurried, and with a stretch of looking up and gazing far, which loosens neck, shoulders, and eyes together. Three to five times a week, forty minutes or so at a time, and stop once a light sweat shows. As for dressing "thick below, light above," the Beginning of Spring and Rain Water essays covered it and there is no call to go over it again; one reminder only: the cold snap that doubles back once spring has begun has not yet passed, so do not hurry to retire the long johns.

## IV. Emotions: Leave the Sentence Half-Said, Leave the Matter Some Room

Spring's qi surges upward, and people grow quick to flare, and in the flare the words come out at full stretch — "you never," "you always," said mostly in the heat of the moment. A day halved evenly between light and dark is just the practice ground for an even mind.

In small, concrete terms: in a quarrel, step out first and pour yourself a glass of water, and give your answer ten minutes later; when emotion is rising, do not rush to reply to the message — finish one small thing at hand first; before sleep, ask whether today you said anything that left no way back — if you did, add a softer word tomorrow. All small things; the difficulty is remembering to do them. The *Suwen* counts "yin and yang parting and severing" the gravest of faults; between one person and another, and between the two ends of an emotion, the same rule holds — do not break away, and do not go too far.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Goji shoots** — the tender early-spring tips of the goji berry bush, barely bitter with a sweetness that returns; blanch them ten-odd seconds in boiling water and dress them cold, or scramble them with eggs. **Celtuce** — the lettuce grown for its thick stem — peeled and cut in shreds, salted lightly for ten minutes and squeezed dry, then dressed with black vinegar and sesame oil; do not discard the leaves: blanch them and toss them in with the rest. **Soybean sprouts** — level in nature; simmer them with tofu or glass noodles into a plain soup, light and grease-cutting, just the thing for the spring-drowsy appetite.
