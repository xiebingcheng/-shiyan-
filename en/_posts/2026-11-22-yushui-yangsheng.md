> 「Good rain knows its season, falling just when spring calls for it; it slips in on the night wind and moistens all things finely, without a sound.」 (「好雨知时节，当春乃发生。随风潜入夜，润物细无声。」)
>
> — **Du Fu**, *"Delighting in Rain on a Spring Night"* (《春夜喜雨》, Tang dynasty)

## I. Only From This Day Does the Rain Truly Fall

Each year on 18–20 February of the Gregorian calendar, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 330°, **Rain Water** (*Yushui*, 雨水) arrives — the second of the twenty-four solar terms, the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. **Wu Cheng** of the Yuan dynasty explains in the ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》):

> 「Rain Water is the mid-node of the first month of the lunar year. Heaven's One gives birth to water: the beginnings of spring belong to wood, yet that which gives birth to wood must be water, and so Rain Water follows Beginning of Spring. And since the east wind has already thawed the frozen ground, the ground loosens and scatters into rain.」

Beginning of Spring only sends word ahead — the ice is still sealed shut. By Rain Water the east wind has warmed the frozen earth loose, and what falls from the sky is finally rain itself. Farmers call spring rain "as precious as oil," and an old saying from Hubei runs: "If Rain Water brings rain, the crops will thrive, and every grain of barley and wheat will fill out plump."

The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — grow livelier pentad by pentad:

- First pentad, "otters offer up fish" (獭祭鱼). "The otter, also named the water-dog, is a stealer of fish; to 'offer fish' is to take the catch and lay it out like a sacrifice to heaven." Feeling the water warm, the fish swim upward, and the otter lines up what it catches in a row, as if performing a rite;
- Second pentad, "the migrant geese turn north" (候雁北). "In the first month of spring the yang qi — the body's warm, active energy — has made its reach, and the migrant geese have turned north from Pengli" — Pengli being the old name of today's Poyang Lake. Yang stirs in the south, and the wild geese swing their heads back north;
- Third pentad, "grass and trees begin to stir" (草木萌动). "The qi of heaven and earth meet and form Tai (the hexagram of peace), and so the grass and trees sprout and stir." Yang qi shoves up through the surface of the soil, and the plants bud.

Sichuan makes a festival of the day. In the west of the province, married daughters return to their parents' home, and parents "pull a baobao" for their children (拉保保) — the local Rain Water custom of finding a child a godfather or godmother — in the hope that "rain and dew nourish, and growth comes easy." All things are fed by rain and dew, and people need watering too: what gets watered here is the spleen and stomach.

## II. Damp Takes the Lead: First Get the Spleen and Stomach Back in the Saddle

After Rain Water, the "rain" that falls into the body is water-damp. How is it to be shifted? The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on the Separate Channels" (经脉别论), lays out the whole account in plain terms:

> 「Drink enters the stomach, and its essence overflows and is carried up to the spleen; the spleen's qi spreads that essence, sending it up to the lung, and the lung keeps the waterways open, passing the flow down to the bladder. Water and essence spread to the four quarters and run side by side through the five channels.」

Water in the belly finds its own way only when the spleen spreads its essence. Let the spleen stop turning, and damp stalls halfway: waking heavy-limbed, a thick greasy tongue coating, sticky stools, no appetite — the words you hear most often after Rain Water. So the keynote of this node is not tonification; it is getting the lead player in moving water and damp — the spleen and stomach — back on its feet.

**Qiu Chuji** of the Yuan dynasty, in the ***Treatise on Life-Cultivation According to the Seasons* · "Messages of Life-Cultivation for Spring"** (《摄生消息论·春季摄生消息》), gives the direction:

> 「In spring, the flavors of food should be lessened in sour and increased in sweet, so as to nourish the qi of the spleen.」

Spring belongs to wood — the phase of surge and growth in the Chinese five-phase scheme — and sour enters the liver; too much of it, and a rampant wood assails earth, the spleen's own phase. Sweet enters the spleen, so the mild, bland-sweet foods are the ones playing the proper part here. Vinegar-glazed and hot-and-sour dishes wait their turn; yam, lotus seed, and Job's-tears (*yiyiren*) step forward.

**Yam and millet porridge**, one bowl at daybreak. Roll-cut 150 g of *tiegun* yam — the slim, dense variety of Chinese yam — into chunks; rinse 60 g of millet clean; add about 1,200 ml of water, bring it to the boil over high heat, then turn down low and simmer for forty minutes, until the "rice oil" — the thin cream that forms on well-cooked congee — floats to the surface. No fresh yam at hand? Use 30 g of dried yam slices, soaked twenty minutes in warm water before they go in; the effect is heavier-handed, and better suited to those with loose stools and a pale tongue with a white coating. Steamed, yam is a snack; simmered into porridge, it is the everyday dish that gives the spleen something to stand on.

**Lotus-seed porridge**, one bowl at night. Take 20 g of lotus seeds, cores removed, and soak them half an hour in warm water; put 50 g of *jingmi* (polished round-grain rice) into the pot with them, add about 1,000 ml of water, and cook until the seeds go soft and floury and the grains bloom open. The lotus seed is sweet, astringent, and level: it supplements the spleen and calms the spirit, and for nights of tossing and turning under steady rain, this is the right bowl. If bitterness bothers you, pick the cores out clean; if you want to clear heart-fire — the Chinese-medicine shorthand for a restless, overheated mind — leave three or five of them in. The seed astringes the bowel, so anyone prone to constipation should use it sparingly.

**Stir-fried Job's-tears water**, through the day in place of tea. Toss 30 g of raw Job's-tears in a dry pan over a low flame until they turn barely gold and the pan smells of toasted grain; leave to cool, then jar them. Each time, take 15 g and steep, covered, in boiling water for twenty minutes, or simmer for ten — one cup a day. If you find it too plain, boil 50 g of *jingmi* with it and eat it as porridge. Raw, Job's-tears are cool in nature: draining damp with ease but fortifying the spleen but little; stir-fried, they turn level and mild — fine to drink every single day through a stretch of rain. They suit best those with a thick greasy tongue coating, a body that feels weighed down, and knees that go heavy on rainy days; use with caution in pregnancy.

## III. Warm One Day, Cold the Next: How to Dress

Beginning of Spring laid out the rhythm for shedding layers: take them off one by one, slowly. At Rain Water the trouble gains another layer, for the cold of this solar term arrives carrying water vapor — a damp cold. Bundle up in a thick coat and you break a sweat; the sweat has nowhere to go, and you have stewed the damp right back into yourself.

Where does damp worm its way in? The *Suwen*'s "Discourse on Taiyin and Yangming" (太阴阳明论) settled the matter long ago:

> 「Those harmed by wind are struck in the upper body first; those harmed by damp are struck in the lower body first.」

So the way of dressing changes, on one rule of thumb: breathe inside, block rain outside. Inner layer breathable — a thin cotton or quick-dry base, so that warming up on a walk does not leave you stewing in your own sweat; middle layer, one lined jacket, swapped as the temperature moves; outer layer rainproof — a trench coat or hooded shell in a crisp fabric that will not drink water. Legs, feet, shoes, and socks matter most of all: Rain Water days make the roads slick, and socks soak through first. Keep a dry pair in the bag, and on reaching home change socks before anything else and set the shoes to dry — a wet shoe shut up overnight is a door left open, for cold-damp enters exactly through the soles. Of spring's six solar terms, Rain Water shows the smallest rise in temperature, and its warming is the least to be trusted: today is warm enough to make you pack the padded coat away; tomorrow the wet cold comes back and takes it out again.

## IV. Spring Rain, Day After Day: Don't Let the Mood Go Moldy

Days on end of grey rain and little light, and the spirits sag — that is not all moodiness; part of it is the season's account. Qiu Chuji wrote the prescription long ago:

> 「On mild and genial spring days, gaze out from gardens, pavilions, and open, airy places, to unstop what is pent up in the breast and set the springing qi in free flow; do not sit inert, for that breeds melancholies of its own.」

"Sit not inert" — do not sit yourself into a wilt at the desk. A few small things, kept up through the rainy days: clear the desk down to what today needs, and the mind loosens with it; add a pothos or a pot of mint to the windowsill — the watering is a trifle, but the days gain something to tend; walk twenty minutes under an umbrella in the light rain, listening only to the drops drumming on the canopy. Du Fu's rain "slips in on the night wind and moistens all things finely, without a sound": rain's nature is to moisten, and the mood, too, opens the same slow way.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Spinach**. Spring spinach is tender and silky; blanch it to draw off the oxalic acid, then dress it cold with sesame oil or drop it into a rolling soup. For those who sit long through rainy days, it keeps the bowels moving.

**Spring bamboo shoots**. Around Rain Water, shoots break soil across Jiangnan, the country south of the lower Yangtze, and "bamboo shoots after rain" could not answer the season better. Slice and blanch before cooking; oil-braised or stir-fried with shredded pork, either serves. Shoots run slippery and hard to digest — for the weak of spleen and stomach, let a taste of the new season be the limit.

**Pea shoots**. At the turn from winter to spring they are crisp and tender; stir-fry them fast over a fierce flame, or blanch a few seconds in the soup and lift them out. Mild enough never to elbow another flavor aside, they sit perfectly beside porridge and broths.
