> 「All things that grow at this time are alike clean, pure, bright, and clear; hence it is called Pure Brightness.」 (「万物生长此时，皆清洁而明净，故谓之清明。」)
>
> — ***Suishi Baiwen*** (《岁时百问》, *A Hundred Questions on the Seasons* — an ancient book of seasonal customs, the original lost; the line survives as quoted in later seasonal literature)

## I. What the Term Means: Two Words — "Clean and Orderly" — Say It All

**Pure Brightness** (*Qingming*, 清明) is the fifth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. Each year on 4 or 5 April of the Gregorian calendar, the sun reaches celestial longitude 15° and the term begins. It follows Beginning of Spring, Rain Water, Awakening of Insects, and the Spring Equinox. And it holds a place none of the others can claim: it is the only solar term that grew into a festival — the tomb-sweeping of the Cold Food Festival (the fireless day before Qingming) and the spring outings of the Shangsi Festival (a third-month festival of waterside outings) were both folded into it in later ages.

The origin of the name is set out fully by Wu Cheng of the Yuan dynasty in the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* (《月令七十二候集解》):

> 「Pure Brightness is the node of the third month. The *Guoyu* — the old *Discourses of the States* — says: the year has its eight winds, and the calendar alone names the clear-bright wind as the wind of the third month's node, because this wind belongs to Xun. All things are brought to full array at Xun, and by this time they are all clean, orderly, bright, and clear.」 (「清明，三月节。按《国语》曰：时有八风，历独指清明风为三月节，此风属巽故也。万物齐乎巽，物至此时皆以洁齐而清明矣。」)

The eight winds are the ancients' classification of the winds by the quarter they blow from, and the clear-bright wind is the one from the southeast; Xun, among the eight trigrams, is the trigram of wind and of the southeast. **"Clean and orderly"** means that by this time every living thing has tidied itself — the trees and grasses shake off the tattered old leaves of late winter and green through, all together, rank upon rank. The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — follow in this order:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **The paulownia begins to blossom** (桐始华) | 「The *tong* is the name of a tree; there are three kinds of it... the one that comes into flower now is the white paulownia.」 (「桐，木名。有三种……今始华者，乃白桐耳。」) |
| **Second** | **The field mouse turns into the *wu* bird** (田鼠化为鴽) | 「Master Bao says: the mouse is a creature of yin, and the *wu* a creature of yang; the yang qi has grown strong, and so the mouse turns into the *wu* — yin transformed by yang.」 (「鲍氏曰：鼠，阴类；鴽，阳类；阳气盛故化为鴽，盖阴为阳所化也。」) |
| **Third** | **The rainbow first appears** (虹始见) | 「It is the qi of yin and yang meeting and crossing... when the clouds thin and let the sun through, and the sunlight strikes the raindrops, the rainbow is born.」 (「是阴阳交会之气……云薄漏日，日照雨滴则虹生焉。」) |

The field mice shun the strengthening sun and slip back into their burrows; the *wu* — a small bird much like a quail — comes out in their stead; and after the rain, the first rainbow hangs in the sky. The Awakening of Insects essay urged readers to eat their shepherd's purse early; by Pure Brightness most of it has already flowered and gone tough, and this spring's first taste of freshness takes its final bow.

## II. Nourishment: A Taste of the Green, a Taste of the Fresh

The festival foods of Pure Brightness hang on two characters: "green" (*qing*) and "fresh" (*xian*). The green is the qingtuan — the green rice ball — and the fresh is the river snail.

**Mugwort qingtuan**. Wash 100 g of tender mugwort (*ai cao*) leaves; add 3 g of baking soda to a pot of boiling water (it keeps the green and lifts out the bitterness), blanch the leaves for thirty seconds, chill them in cold water and squeeze them dry, then blend them with 150 ml of clean water into a fine green paste. Mix 200 g of glutinous rice flour, 50 g of wheat starch, and 15 g of sugar; pour in the mugwort paste while it is still boiling hot and knead into a smooth green dough, then knead in 10 g of oil until it is worked through. Divide 240 g of red-bean paste into twelve portions; take 35 g of dough for each wrapper, wrap and seal the balls, set them on oiled paper in the steamer, and steam for ten minutes once the water comes to a full boil. Brush a coat of oil over them as they come out so they will not stick, and eat them while just warm, when they are at their softest. Where fresh mugwort cannot be had — as in much of the north — use *maiqing*, the pressed juice of young wheat greens, instead; the method is otherwise the same. The *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*) of Li Shizhen of the Ming, in its "Herbs" section under mugwort, says of the leaf that 「raw it is warm, cooked it is hot — it is pure yang」 (「生温熟热，纯阳也」): the herb leans warming by nature, and its juice kneaded into the dough is just the thing while the spring chill lingers. But glutinous rice is sticky and slow to digest, and harder still once cold — re-steam a leftover qingtuan before eating it, keep to one or two a day, and if the spleen and stomach are weak, stop at a taste.

**River snails stir-fried in soybean paste**. The folk proverb runs, "At Pure Brightness the river snail rivals a plump goose" — around the term the water is still cool, the snails have only just woken from the silt, and the meat is at its fattest. Keep 500 g of river snails in clean water for two days, with a few drops of sesame oil added to spur them to spit out the mud; before cooking, scrub them clean and snip off the tail tips. Mince 15 g of ginger; take three cloves of garlic; seven or eight perilla leaves (scallion sections will do if there are none); 15 g of soybean paste. Heat a more generous measure of oil than usual in the wok, sauté the ginger and garlic until fragrant, fry the soybean paste until the red oil comes out, add the snails and stir-fry over high heat for two minutes, splash in 15 ml of cooking wine, add half a small bowl of hot water, cover and simmer for three minutes, then add the perilla and reduce the sauce. Two things brook no carelessness: first, cook them through — nothing goes in the mouth while any raw juices are left; second, snip the tails and discard the innards, eating only the meat at the head end. The snail leans cold by nature; ginger and perilla both lift the fragrance and offset the cold, and those whose spleen and stomach run weak and cold should stop after a few.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: Heavy Damp and Heavy Grief

「In the Pure Brightness season the rain falls in drizzles」 (「清明时节雨纷纷」) — Du Mu's line has said all there is to say about Pure Brightness in Jiangnan, the country south of the lower Yangtze. The more it rains, the heavier the damp. The signals of heavy damp are specific: opening your eyes in the morning to a body that feels leaden, as though dressed in clothes that never got wrung out; a sticky taste in the mouth; a tongue coating thicker than usual. Rein the diet in first — push the rich, sweet, and greasy, and anything chilled or iced, to the back of the queue; add two or three slices of ginger to the stir-fry; pair the meal with a bowl of hot porridge. After eating, take a brisk walk along the river embankment for thirty to forty minutes, until a light sweat shows — the damp scatters with the sweat. In the north, the season more often brings wind than rain: the damp stays light, but the throat dries out first, and the rock-sugar stewed pear from the Awakening of Insects essay can stay on the menu.

On the days when the tombs are swept, the emotions rise and fall — the common lot of being human, and no one need pretend otherwise. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Treatise on Pain" (《素问·举痛论》), counts 「all illnesses as born of qi」 (「百病生于气」), and grief is one of the movers; 「when grief comes, the qi is consumed」 (「悲则气消」). Grief beyond measure spends the qi faster than anything, so coming home from the graves feeling worn out is not affectation. If the tears want to come, let them come — tears were always the way out. Do not sit alone with it when you return; share a hot meal with the family. Tell the children the old stories of those who came before — remembrance spoken aloud weighs less than remembrance pressed down inside. And if the low spell runs past two weeks, with eating and sleeping both thrown into disorder, take it seriously, as you would any ordinary illness, and ask for help in good time — there is nothing to be embarrassed about.

## IV. Daily Life: From "Thick Below, Light Above" to "Light, but Prepared"

**"Thick below, light above"** can begin to wind down at Pure Brightness. The Awakening of Insects essay said to give the long johns another half month; the Spring Equinox essay advised not hurrying to retire them; and the folk proverb promises that "Pure Brightness ends the snow, and Grain Rain ends the frost" — with frost and snow done for the year, the lower body may shed a layer. Winding it down is not the same as going over to single spring layers: the late spring cold snap — the chill that doubles back once spring has begun — still has one parting thrust in it, so keep a thin coat at hand for mornings and evenings. After rain the temperature falls fastest of all, and walking on cold ground in wet shoes does real harm; the old should still wait for the sun to climb before going out.

The *Suwen*, in its "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》), says that in the three months of spring one should 「lie down at night and rise early, walk at ease in the courtyard, let the hair hang loose and the body relax, and so let the will come to life」 (「夜卧早起，广步于庭，被发缓形，以使志生」) — loosen one's clothing, slow the stride, and let the emotions rise with the spring qi. At Pure Brightness, the footnote to these lines is the outing into the spring green. The people of the Song scouted the route for us long ago: the *Dongjing Meng Hua Lu* (《东京梦华录》, *Memories of the Northern Song Capital*), in its seventh chapter, records Pure Brightness in the old Northern Song capital of Bianjing: 「The open country on every side was like a marketplace; people would make for the shade of the flowering trees, or into the gardens and parks, spread out their cups and dishes in rows, and press one another to drink」 (「四野如市，往往就芳树之下，或园囿之间，罗列杯盘，互相劝酬。」) — and did not turn home 「until dusk」 (「抵暮而归」). None of that display is asked of us: forty minutes on the park lawn or along the tree-shaded embankment is enough. After rain the mountain paths are slick, so the old should carry a staff; pollen and willow catkins are at their height, so those prone to allergy should wear a mask, and on getting home wash the face and rinse the mouth first. Smoothing the liver takes no medicine: take whatever is pent up out into the open, and spend the time watching far trees and running water — better for the liver qi than curling up on the sofa with a phone.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Mingqian tea** — the first picking of spring tea, taken before Pure Brightness, with buds and leaves just beginning to unfurl and the bitter, astringent edge still light; what one drinks it for is a single word: freshness. Steep 3 g of leaf in 150 ml of water at about 85 °C; pour and drink at once, and never let it sit until it turns strong; after midday, go easy on it, or the night's sleep will suffer. **River mussels** — at their fattest around Pure Brightness; rub the mussel meat with salt to work off the slime, simmer it first with slices of ginger and cooking wine to take away the muddy taste, then add the tofu and stew them together for a quarter of an hour, until the soup turns milky white, and scatter white pepper over it as it comes off the fire. **Pea shoots** — the tender tips of the newly sprouted pea; pinch the tips and stir-fry plain — hot wok, little oil, minced garlic until fragrant, one minute over high heat and out — crisp, tender, clean and sweet; dropped into soup as an accompaniment, they do just as well.
