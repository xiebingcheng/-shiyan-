> 「Greater Snow, the node of the eleventh month. *Da* means abundance: from here on, the snow is abundant.」 (「大雪，十一月节。大者，盛也。至此而雪盛矣。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. Yin Reaches the Top, and Yang Starts to Stir

Each year on 6–8 December of the Gregorian calendar, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 255°, **Greater Snow** (*Daxue*, 大雪) arrives — the twenty-first of the twenty-four solar terms, the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. On the lunar calendar that still steers these nodes, this is the eleventh month, the one the ancients called midwinter; from here on, snow carries weight.

Read in sequence, the three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — trace one hidden thread: yin at its extreme gives birth to yang.

- First pentad, "the hedan falls silent" (鹖鴠不鸣). The hedan is the creature folk call hanhaochong, the "cry-all-night" creature — no bird at all, in fact, but the complex-toothed flying squirrel. The old gloss says it is "by nature a yang bird, yet it falls silent at the very extreme of yin": when the cold is deepest, even this all-night crier goes mute;
- Second pentad, "tigers begin to mate" (虎始交). The *Monthly Ordinances*: "The tiger is a fierce beast — hence the materia medica says it can repel evil spirits and spectral harm — and now, feeling the faint yang qi ever more keenly, they come together in mating." Right when yin qi is at its fullest, the tiger is the first to catch the scent of newborn yang;
- Third pentad, "the li sprouts" (荔挺出). The li is the maxie, a plant of the malan-flower kind — a wild iris. It too feels the stirring of yang, and pushes a shoot up through the frozen ground.

All this phenology — the old habit of reading the season by what beasts and plants do — really says one thing: when yin reaches the summit, yang begins to move. Out of doors, soy-cured meats and salted fish gather under the eaves in greater numbers by the day: "Pickle the vegetables at Lesser Snow; cure the meat at Greater Snow" — once the cured meats go up on their racks, the New Year cannot be far. Indoors, we ought to follow that glimmer of new yang too, and begin storing up reserves in the body. How to store it — that is a matter of rhythm.

## II. The Rhythm of Winter Tonification: Rotate, Don't Gorge

"Tonify in winter and you may wrestle a tiger come spring," the old saying goes — grandly. But the weight of it rests on those last two words, "come spring." Tonification — *jinbu*, the Chinese practice of banking bodily strength through the cold months with rich, warming food — aims at a whole winter's stamina, not the flourish of a single feast. Gorge on a mutton hotpot tonight and by morning your mouth is dry and blistered, your belly bloated and slow to settle; for half a month you do not dare touch meat. That is not tonifying; that is torment. Those who know how to do it save like careful savers: regular deposits, small sums, never a break in the line.

Around Greater Snow, three kinds of food take their turn.

**Soup**, twice a week. Black-bone chicken and red date soup: half a silkie chicken — the black-skinned, black-boned fowl prized in Chinese soup pots — about 750 g, put into a pot of cold water and blanched clean of blood-scum; 8 red dates (jujubes), split open and pitted, plus 3 slices of ginger; everything into a clay pot, filled with plenty of hot water, brought to a boil over high heat, then turned down to a low simmer for an hour and a half; salt only in the last ten minutes before it comes off the fire, and it is done when the sweetness of the dates has been stewed into the broth. The red date holds a place in the top grade of the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (《神农本草经》, the oldest surviving Chinese pharmacopeia): "The great jujube is sweet and level. It governs evil qi of the heart and abdomen; it settles the center and nourishes the spleen, aids the twelve channels, levels the stomach qi, opens the nine apertures, supplements scant qi and scant fluids, addresses insufficiency within the body, great fright, and heaviness of the limbs, and harmonizes the hundred medicines. Taken long, it lightens the body and lengthens the years." That one phrase, "settles the center and nourishes the spleen," speaks directly to the idleness of the spleen and stomach in winter.

**Congee**, on alternate days. Red date, longan, and glutinous rice congee: 80 g of glutinous rice, 10 g of dried longan flesh — longan being the sweet "dragon-eye" fruit of southern China, usually sold dried — and 5 red dates; add about 1200 ml of water, simmer forty minutes over a low fire, and wait for the "rice oil" — the thin cream that forms on well-cooked congee — to float to the surface. In the *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, the *Compendium of Materia Medica*), Li Shizhen set longan beside lychee: "As food the lychee is prized, but for nourishment the longan is better — the lychee is hot in nature, while the longan is mild and even." Yet "mild and even" is a verdict passed only relative to lychee: longan is warming after all, and eaten by the handful it raises inner heat just the same; five or six a day in the congee is the steady course. Cao Tingdong of the Qing dynasty, opening the "Discourse on the Porridge Spectrum" of his *Lao Lao Heng Yan* (《老老恒言》, *Everyday Words for the Aged*), writes: "Congee can benefit a person; for the old it is most fitting of all." The words were addressed to the elderly, but when the spleen and stomach dawdle in winter, everyone may share the good of this bowl.

**Root vegetables**, the staple's best mates. Steam sweet potatoes and let one replace half a bowl of rice at a meal; peel taro, cut it into chunks, and stew it with pork ribs; stir-fry carrots in oil until soft before they go into the soup, and the body absorbs them better. Root vegetables grow anchored in the earth, thick with the richness of the soil: filling, gently nourishing, and they leave the rich meat dishes room to breathe.

To rotate means: soup today, congee the day after, roots underneath it all — meat and greens, dry dishes and broths staggered, so the stomach and bowels have work to do every day and overtime on none. If a cold with fever comes on, or the tongue puts on a thick, greasy coat, pause the tonics first, and let the body settle what is in front of it before starting again.

## III. Daily Life: Guard the Head and the Back

The *Nanjing* (《难经》, the *Classic of Difficulties*), at the forty-seventh difficulty, states: "The head is the meeting ground of all yang." The three yang channels of the hand and the three of the foot — channels being the pathways along which Chinese medicine charts the flow of qi — all climb upward to converge on the head and face: yang qi, the body's warm, active energy, is at its fullest there, and heat escapes fastest. One gust of wind on a snowy day and most of your warmth is gone through the crown; outdoors, a hat buys more than an extra sweater. In the north, rooms are baked by central heating and the head sweats — all the more reason to guard it on the way out; in the south, damp cold works into the bone, and neither hat nor scarf may be spared.

Do not slack on the back, either. A thin cotton-padded vest worn against the skin works better than a heavy overcoat — let the back catch cold, and a cough soon follows. For morning exercise, wait until the sun is up; snow before sunrise is cold and slick, and one fall is not worth it.

The nights are long; let sleep stretch a little with them, but do not lounge in bed all morning. A hot-water bottle tucked under the covers before you lie down feels more solid than an electric blanket left on all night; leave the bedroom window cracked a little for air, and sleeping with the head hidden under the quilt is the one habit not to keep. Sleep seven or eight full hours at night; if drowsiness weighs on the day, a twenty-minute nap after lunch beats gritting it out until dark.

## IV. Long Nights, Warm Lamps: Let the Mind Lie Hidden

The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit," says the winter mind should be "made to lie as if hidden, as if stored away — as if holding some private intent, as if already possessed of what it sought": the emotions like seeds buried in the soil, gathered in, tucked under, making no show. This is not an order to shut oneself up; it is permission to use the long nights for some quiet work of one's own.

After supper, switch on a lamp of warm yellow light and read a few pages of a paper book — gentler nourishment than the din of short videos. Keep the room both bright and soft: when dark falls early, turn the lamps fully on and do not pinch pennies on electricity; a dim room only draws a person down toward low spirits. Lie down around half past ten, and let sleep outrun the thought of staying up. Winter leaves people alone more often; the soft rustle of pages turning is the best footnote there is to that word "hidden."

## V. Four Foods of the Season

Warm tonification is one side of the ledger; winter dryness and the stagnation that rich food brings are the other. In heated rooms lips peel and stools turn dry — cooling, moistening foods are what shore up the base.

**Napa cabbage (baicai).** "Of a hundred vegetables, none matches the baicai." Stewed with tofu or flash-fried with vinegar, it suits any table, and its rough fiber sweeps clean the gut that all the winter tonics keep busy.

**Water chestnut.** Peel it and boil it in one pot with sugarcane; let it cool to lukewarm and drink it as tea — it clears heat and generates fluids, just the thing for the parched mouth that heated rooms produce. The water chestnut grows as an aquatic plant, so eating it cooked through is safer than raw.

**Sugarcane.** Cut purple-skinned cane into sections and boil it in water with two red dates added: sweet, not cloying. The old and the young, chewing it, should mind their teeth against the hard fiber.

**Winter bamboo shoots.** Shredded and stir-fried with cured pork (*larou*), they are a fixture of winter in Jiangnan, the country south of the lower Yangtze. Blanch them first to draw off the astringency; one small plate at a time is plenty. Bamboo shoots lean cold in nature; those with a weak spleen and stomach should take no more than a taste.
