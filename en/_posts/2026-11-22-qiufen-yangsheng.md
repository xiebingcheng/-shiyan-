> 「The Autumn Equinox is where yin and yang each take their half; hence day and night are divided evenly and cold and heat share the scales.」 (「秋分者，阴阳相半也，故昼夜均而寒暑平。」)
>
> — **Dong Zhongshu**, ***Chunqiu Fanlu · "Yin and Yang Going Out and In, Above and Below"*** (《春秋繁露·阴阳出入上下》, *Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals*, Western Han dynasty)

## I. What the Term Means: Autumn Takes Its Half Here

**The Autumn Equinox** (*Qiufen*, 秋分) is the sixteenth of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round — and the fourth of autumn. Each year on 22 to 24 September of the Gregorian calendar, the sun reaches celestial longitude 180° and the term begins. On this day the sun stands directly over the equator, so that day and night are of equal length the world over — and autumn's ninety days here stand at their half.

Wu Cheng of the Yuan puts it with the utmost economy in the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads* (《月令七十二候集解》):

> 「The Autumn Equinox is the mid-node of the eighth month. For the explanation, see the Spring Equinox.」 (「秋分，八月中。解见春分。」)

The gloss sends the reader back to the Spring Equinox entry — 「'Fen' means the half」 (「分者，半也」) — two margin notes gazing at each other from across half a year. The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — follow in this order:

| Pentad | Phenomenon | Explanation (after the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*) |
|--------|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **First** | **Thunder first falls silent** (雷始收声) | 「Master Bao says: thunder sounds in the second month, amid the yang, and falls silent in the eighth month, amid the yin; when it enters the earth, the ten thousand things enter along with it.」 (「鲍氏曰：雷，二月阳中发声，八月阴中收声，入地则万物随入也。」) |
| **Second** | **The hibernating insects plaster their burrows shut** (蛰虫坯户) | 「The mud washed from making tiles is called *pei* — fine clay.」 (「淘瓦之泥曰坯，细泥也。」) — the hibernating insects paste fine clay over their small burrow mouths against the cold |
| **Third** | **The waters begin to dry up** (水始涸) | 「The commentary on the *Book of Rites* says: water is at bottom the work of qi; in spring and summer the qi arrives and so the waters swell, in autumn and winter the qi withdraws and so they run dry.」 (「《礼记》注曰：水本气之所为，春夏气至故长，秋冬气返故涸也。」) |

At the Spring Equinox thunder first sounds; at the Autumn Equinox it first falls silent — one a sounding, one a silencing, set symmetrically half a year apart. Burrows pasted shut, waters drawing down: everything is gathering in.

The Autumn Equinox was once the "Moon-Offering Festival": the *Book of Rites* (《礼记》) records that 「the Son of Heaven pays morning court to the sun in spring and offers evening sacrifice to the moon in autumn」 (「天子春朝日，秋夕月」) — the Altar of the Moon just outside Beijing's Fuchengmen is where the Ming and Qing emperors performed that evening rite. But the equinox moon is so often not full that the sacrifice was later moved on to the Mid-Autumn Festival, where the whole etiquette of reunion flourished the more. And since 2018 the Autumn Equinox has carried one more name besides, the Chinese Farmers' Harvest Festival — on this day, eating something good has full warrant. The luck that egg balancing (*shudan*) courts was told in the Spring Equinox essay; stand the egg up once more at the Autumn Equinox.

For health, the governing idea is still the one word "balance." The Spring Equinox essay quoted the *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), from its "Treatise on the Vital Qi That Communicates with Heaven," the line 「when yin lies calm and yang holds secure」 (「阴平阳秘」); the Autumn Equinox takes up its sister line, from the "Great Treatise on the Utmost Truth and the Major Essentials" (《素问·至真要大论》): 「Watch with care where yin and yang lie, and regulate them, taking balance as the aim.」 (「谨察阴阳所在而调之，以平为期。」) **Take balance as the aim** — eat nothing that leans too far cold or too far hot, keep no schedule that runs to extremes, and let no mood surge and sink unchecked. Only, what heaven and earth advanced after the Spring Equinox — the capital of growth and unfolding — is now to be drawn back in, from the Autumn Equinox on.

## II. Nourishment: A Bowl of Duck Soup, a Steamer of Plain-Steamed Crab

Lingnan — the far southern country around Guangdong — keeps an old custom of gathering "autumn greens" and boiling them into "autumn soup": the autumn greens are a wild amaranth, known locally as *qiubi hao*; on the day of the Autumn Equinox the whole village goes out to the field ridges to pick them and rolls them with fish slices into a soup. The folk rhyme runs, "autumn soup fills and rinses the organs clean, from liver to gut; a whole household, old and young, is safe and well" — word for word the lucky phrase quoted in the Spring Equinox essay. The spring soup rinsed away the stale old qi; the autumn soup moistens away the weariness that follows the harvest in.

**Old-duck soup with glehnia root and polygonatum**. After the Autumn Equinox the dryness is still here — only it has turned from warm to cool. Stew a bowl of old-duck soup: moistening, and not greasy. Take half an old duck, chopped into chunks (about 600 g), set it in a pot of cold water with 15 ml of cooking wine, bring it to the boil, skim off the scum, lift it out and rinse it clean. Rinse 15 g of glehnia root (*shashen*) and 15 g of polygonatum (*yuzhu*), and set out three slices of ginger. Fill a clay pot with about 1,500 ml of boiling water, add the duck, the glehnia, the polygonatum, and the ginger, bring it back to the boil over high heat, turn down to a low simmer, and stew an hour and a half; ten minutes before taking it off the fire, stir in 3 g of salt. The *Suixiju Yinshipu* (《随息居饮食谱》, *A Dietary Manual of the Restful Dwelling*) of Wang Mengying of the Qing records the duck as 「sweet and cool」 (「甘凉」), able to 「nourish the yin of the five *zang* organs, clear the heat of deficiency and overwork, replenish the blood and set the waters moving, and settle the stomach while engendering fluids」 (「滋五脏之阴，清虚劳之热，补血行水，养胃生津」), and adds that 「the drake, plump, large, and very old, is the good one」 (「雄而肥大极老者良」). The soup wants an old duck, then — a tender one will not give that flavor — and the three slices of ginger are there just to pull the coolness back a step. Glehnia root and polygonatum are stocked at any pharmacy, long-standing partners in moistening dryness; anyone in the midst of a cold or a run of diarrhea should let this soup wait for another day.

**Plain-steamed crab**. The saying goes: "When the autumn wind rises, the crab's legs grow hard." Folk practice keeps the rule of "nine female, ten male" — in the ninth lunar month the hen crab, round of apron, stands rich with roe, in the tenth the cock, pointed of apron, stands thick with paste — and the Autumn Equinox arrives right at the gate of that season. Choose four or five live crabs (about 600 g); leave their ties on and scrub the seams of the shells clean with a soft brush, then turn each one belly-up and lay them in the steamer tray on two slices of perilla or ginger — belly-up, so that the roe does not run out. Once the water boils, steam twelve to fifteen minutes over high heat; the shells must go red clean through before they count as done. For the dipping sauce, take the Suzhou way: 20 g of minced ginger, 30 ml of aromatic vinegar (the black rice vinegar of Jiangnan), 2 g of sugar. A few rules brook no shortcuts: never a dead crab — the moment its proteins spoil, the histamine shoots up; freshwater crabs must be steamed through and through, against the lung fluke (a parasite); and the ginger, the vinegar, and the perilla lift the freshness and press the cold down at the same time. The *Bencao Gangmu* (《本草纲目》, *Compendium of Materia Medica*) of Li Shizhen of the Ming, in its "Shells" section (介部), records the crab as 「salty, cold, and of small toxicity」 (「咸，寒，有小毒」), and Shizhen added his warning: 「It must not be eaten together with persimmon or schizonepeta — it provokes sudden purging disorders and stirs up the wind ailments; the juice of costus root can undo it.」 (「不可同柿及荆芥食，发霍乱动风，木香汁可解」) Persimmons are just now coming to market as well — two things of great cold; keep them apart on the tongue. Two crabs to a sitting is the right measure; those whose stomachs run cold and loose easily, and anyone in an acute gout flare, should stop at a taste — or let it be altogether.

## III. Tending What the Season Brings: Cool Dryness and the Autumn Blues

At White Dew the dryness was warm dryness — a dry throat, nostrils breathing fire, phlegm sticky and hard to bring up. After the Autumn Equinox, the dryness changes its temper. Wu Jutong's *Wenbing Tiaobian* (《温病条辨》, *Treatise on Warm Diseases*) divides autumn dryness into warm and cool: **cool dryness** sets in after the Autumn Equinox — a dry cough, phlegm thin and white, a blocked nose, a dry throat with little wish to drink, a body that shrinks from the cold, no sweat. The *Wenbing Tiaobian*, in its "Supplement on the Prevailing Qi of Autumn Dryness" (《温病条辨·补秋燥胜气论》), puts the case squarely: 「When dryness wounds its own organ, the head aches slightly, one shrinks from the cold, the cough brings up thin phlegm, the nose is stopped, the throat feels barred, the pulse is string-taut, and there is no sweat — Xingsu Powder governs it.」 (「燥伤本脏，头微痛，恶寒，咳嗽稀痰，鼻塞，嗌塞，脉弦，无汗，杏苏散主之。」) Xingsu Powder (apricot kernel, perilla leaf, aged tangerine peel, and their kind) is the orthodox formula for cool dryness, and it must be prescribed by a physician — the Sangxing Decoction of the Start of Autumn essay treats warm dryness; aimed the wrong way round, it would be cold laid upon cold. Where diet can help is in plain, solid small things: take the soups and the porridges hot, add two or three slices of ginger to the stew pot, and let the raw and chilled melons and fruits wait a while — this is the season when the spleen and stomach want their food cooked through and warm.

The autumn blues call for guarding too. After the Autumn Equinox the daylight shortens by the day — by a little past five in the afternoon the light is already going down — and many a mood goes down with it. The ancients called it the sadness of autumn (*beiqiu*); it is the rhythm of light that has changed, not an affectation. The things one can do are not hard: take twenty minutes of sun around ten in the morning; before dark, walk briskly for half an hour — and ask someone along, which beats scrolling your phone alone; and by day, do not pull the curtains fully shut. If the low spell runs past two weeks and eating and sleeping have both come unhinged, go and register at a clinic as you would for any ordinary complaint. The Pure Brightness essay said that grief must be given somewhere to rest; the autumn blues are the same — give it an outlet, and give it a road back.

## IV. Daily Life: Autumn Freezing, Now Done with Restraint

"Nights of White Dew and the Autumnal Equinox — each night colder than the last." By the Autumn Equinox the nighttime cool has become a settled fact. The old saying goes on from there: "At White Dew bare not the body; at Cold Dew bare not the feet." White Dew guards the upper body; Cold Dew guards the ankles; the Autumn Equinox, caught between the two, is the node at which **autumn freezing** (*qiu dong*, the autumn-side counterpart of spring covering — the old rule of keeping the winter layers on into spring) turns to freezing with restraint: by day the body may still be allowed to grow slowly used to the cool; for going out in the morning and evening, add a thin jacket; sleep with the window closed but a slit left open; and belly, back, and ankles are the first to be guarded. The old, the young, and those weak of body need not court the cold on purpose. As for where autumn freezing ends, the End of Heat essay drew the line long since: after Frost's Descent, freeze no more.

The daily schedule follows the nights as they lengthen. The *Suwen*'s "Great Treatise on the Four Qi and the Regulation of the Spirit" (《素问·四气调神大论》) gives autumn its eight characters — 「retire early and rise early, with the roosters」 (「早卧早起，与鸡俱兴」); the Start of Autumn essay quoted the whole passage — get into bed half an hour earlier than at the End of Heat, and let sleep chase the night as it goes. Exercise, too, stays on the side of gathering: brisk walking, easy jogging, one run-through of the Baduanjin — the "Eight Pieces of Brocade," the classic qigong sequence — and stop once a light sweat shows; sweat streaming down in floods, and the yang qi only just gathered would scatter out again.

## V. Three Foods of the Season

**Pomegranate**. Around the Autumn Equinox the seed grains swell to fullness; crack the fruit open and eat it as it comes, half a fruit at a time. Sweet-sour and heavy with juice, a few segments after a meal cut the grease of richer dishes; pressed into juice and thinned with warm water serves just as well.

**Grapes**. They are at their sweetest right about the Autumn Equinox; wash them and eat them straight off the whole bunch, one small bunch at a time; a small handful of raisins stirred into porridge sweetens it by nature alone.

**Ginkgo nuts** (*baiguo*). The seed kernels of the ginkgo tree, coming down in showers around the Autumn Equinox. The *Bencao Gangmu*, in its "Fruits" section (果部), records the ginkgo nut as 「when cooked, slightly bitter with a faint sweetness, warm in nature and of small toxicity」 (「熟食，小苦微甘，性温有小毒」), and quotes Wu Rui's word of warning: 「Children who eat much of it grow dazed and vomit and purge; it brings on fright convulsions and draws on infantile malnutrition.」 (「小儿食多昏霍，发惊引疳」) So the rules must stay hard: never eat it raw; it must be boiled through or fried done; the green germ at the core must be picked out; three to five nuts a day for a child, about ten for an adult. Boil them in salted water, or drop a handful into the pot with stewed chicken — only when the cooking is fully done may one eat them with an easy mind.
