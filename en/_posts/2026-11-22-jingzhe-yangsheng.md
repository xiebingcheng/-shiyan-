>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. The Thunder Sounds, and Everything Is Called Awake, One After Another

**Awakening of Insects** (*Jingzhe*, 惊蛰) is the third of the twenty-four solar terms — the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round. Each year on 5 or 6 March of the Gregorian calendar, the sun reaches celestial longitude 345° and the term begins. It was originally called *Qizhe* (启蛰), "the stirring of the hibernators"; the usual account has it that in the Han dynasty the name was changed to "Jingzhe" to avoid the taboo on Emperor Jing's given name — the old rule that an emperor's personal name might not be spoken or written. The name changed; the meaning did not: what has lain hidden all winter is called awake by the thunder.

The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — all tell of waking. First pentad, "the peach begins to blossom" (桃始华): "the peach, a fruit tree red of flower, opens its first blooms in this month." Second pentad, "the canggeng sings" (仓庚鸣): "'Geng' is also written with the bird radical; the bird meant is the huangli, the yellow oriole" — and the oriole finds its voice. Third pentad, "the hawk turns into a dove" (鹰化为鸠): "the dove is the bugu of today — the cuckoo" — once the cuckoo calls, it is time to press on with the ploughing. (All three glosses quoted from the *Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*.)

Plants wake, insects wake, and people wake with them. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine) says in its "Treatise on the Viscera-Qi and the Four Seasons" (《素问·脏气法时论》) that "the liver governs spring": in spring the liver qi is the qi on duty, and qi and blood are moving outward, like buds on the branch that are green in promise but not yet in color. But wake too abruptly and there is trouble of its own: when liver qi surges upward too fast, it turns into liver fire; and early spring, with its much wind and little rain, dries out the throat and eyes first whenever a gust passes. Strip everything else away, and staying well at Awakening of Insects comes down to two things: keep the liver qi smooth, and keep the lung moist.

## II. Eating Pears at Awakening of Insects: A Word for "Parting," a Mouthful of Moisture

The old saying goes: "Eat a pear at Awakening of Insects, and you will be in fine spirits all year." The proverb makes its rounds through northern Jiangsu and across Shanxi. Why the pear, of all fruits? The usual explanation is a pun: *li*, the pear, sounds exactly like *li*, "to part." Eat one on this day and the hope is that the crop pests will part from the fields and the aches and pains will part from the body. Shanxi tells another story, this one tied to *zou xikou* — "going out through the western passes," the old migration of Shanxi folk beyond the Great Wall in search of a living: as a son of the house set out on the long road, his family handed him a pear — "part from home," it said, "but never from your roots." Legend or not, the pear itself is exactly the right fruit for the season.

Li Zhongzi of the Ming, in his *Bencao Tongxuan* (《本草通玄》, *Penetrating the Mysteries of the Materia Medica*), writes of the pear: "Sweet in flavor and cold in nature. It moistens the lung and cools the heart, disperses phlegm and drives fire downward, checks cough and quenches thirst. Raw, it clears the heat of the six *fu* — the hollow, draining organs of Chinese medicine; cooked, it nourishes the yin of the five *zang* — the solid, storing ones." One sentence, two roads: raw, the pear leans cool, for those with dry throats, thirst, and a red tongue; stewed, it leans moist, and even a weak spleen and stomach can take it. For dry spring throats and coughing that wakes one at night, the second road is the one most people take.

Rock-sugar stewed pear is simple to make: one snow pear, about 250 g, peeled, cored, and cut into chunks; 10 g of rock sugar; water just enough to cover the pear; bring to a boil over high heat, then turn down to a low simmer and stew for 20 minutes, and eat it warm, broth and fruit together. In the north, where the district heating is not shut off until late and the indoor air is dry, it is especially apt. Those who like their pear pressed should dilute the juice with an equal part of warm water — a small half-cup at a time is plenty. Those whose spleen and stomach are weak and cold, with loose, unformed stools, should keep both raw pear and pear juice at arm's length and take theirs stewed instead.

A pear alone is not the whole answer; add one companion, white fungus (*Yin'er*, tremella — the pale jelly fungus of Chinese kitchens). Take about 10 g of dried white fungus, soak it in cold water for two hours, tear it into small florets, add about 1,000 ml of water, and simmer over a low fire for 40 minutes, until the soup turns faintly thick and glossy with the fungus's natural gelatin; just before taking it off the heat, add 10 g of rock sugar and a dozen or so wolfberries. Cook it the day before, and a bowl the next morning goes down softer on the throat than coffee.

## III. Spring Dryness and Liver Fire: The Eyes and the Temper Know It First

Before liver fire properly flares, the body sends advance notice: a dry throat in the morning; eyes dry, gritty, and red by afternoon; a shorter fuse than usual; nights spent tossing and turning instead of sleeping. None of it amounts to illness — it is a note handed over by the solar term, and once you have read it, the rest is easy.

The measures are plain. Be asleep before eleven at night — liver blood is replenished through the hours of sleep, and the later you stay up, the bigger the fire grows. Set the spicy food and the barbecue aside for the moment. Keep chrysanthemum tea on hand: 3 g of Hangbaiju — the white chrysanthemum grown around Hangzhou (five or six blooms) — steeped in boiling water, with a dozen or so wolfberries if you like; a cup in the afternoon when the eyes are dry and the head feels tight. Those whose stomach runs cold should not drink it strong on an empty stomach.

The same *Suwen* treatise carries one more line: "The liver desires dispersal; hasten to feed it the acrid, that it may disperse." Liver qi loves to unfurl, and the mildly acrid kitchen greens — scallions, Chinese chives, cilantro — help it rise. But mildly acrid is exactly what it says: not chili, not Sichuan pepper. When liver fire is already burning, more spice is oil on the flames.

## IV. Dressing and Getting Up: The Warming Has Quickened, and the Covering Must Change With It

The Beginning of Spring essay took up **spring covering** (*chun wu*, the old rule of keeping the winter layers on well past the calendar's turn to spring), and its whole art hung on one word: wait. By Awakening of Insects the warming has visibly quickened, and the covering needs a small adjustment. The old saying runs **"thick below, light above"**: the upper body may change into lighter shirts early, but the lower half should not be hurried — keep the long johns on for another half month, and do not rush to swap the shoes for thin-soled ones. With feet and legs kept warm, one hardly ever catches a chill.

Even on days when the afternoon high touches 20 °C, do not cut down to the bottom in one go. Two layers when you head out in the morning; shed the outer one when noon turns warm; put it back on when the evening cools — dressing and undressing two or three times in a day is perfectly normal. And do not spring straight out of bed: on waking, stretch first — arms and legs pulled out long — then sit up and get up, and take two or three minutes of stretching by the window. In spring the body has only just woken; it is like dough, and must be left to rise in its own time.

## V. Keeping Anger in Check: Give It Three Seconds

**The liver governs the free flow of qi** — the smooth passage of vital energy through the whole body. In spring the liver qi is already on its way out; add one more obstruction and something gives: dammed up in the chest it is a weight on the chest; bursting out, it is anger. That is why the ancients counted restraint from anger in spring a matter of particular importance.

In practice it comes down to three small things. Wait three seconds before you speak, and swallow half of any angry sentence before it leaves the mouth. Go out into the afternoon sun and walk a loop around the housing compound or along the street — ten minutes or so, phone away, until your back has warmed faintly. And when there is real fire with nowhere to go, write it down, walk two more laps, and come back to talk it over. The *Suwen* has a line for this too: "The liver suffers from tension and haste; hasten to feed it the sweet, that it may be eased." The liver abhors being wound tight, and the sweet and moistening can settle it — at such a moment, a bowl of warm stewed pear works better than an argument.

## VI. Three Foods of the Season

**Shepherd's purse** — most tender before Qingming (Pure Brightness, the tomb-sweeping festival in early April, about a month after Awakening of Insects): pinch off the tender tips that have not yet flowered, blanch them, then stir-fry plain or drop them into soup. **Water chestnut** (*ma ti*) — folk call it "the snow pear underground"; peel it and boil it in water: clear-sweet, moistening for dryness, yet not cold on the palate — it grows in the water, so eating it cooked is the surer way. **Lily bulb** — 20 g of dried lily bulb, soaked an hour in advance and simmered along with the white-fungus soup, adds one more measure of moistening to the lung.

Shepherd's purse is already at the markets in the south; the north will wait another half month yet. When you see it, buy it — do not wait for it to flower.
