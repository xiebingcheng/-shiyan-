> 「Winter Solstice, the middle node of the eleventh month. The qi of closing and storing reaches its utmost here.」 (「冬至，十一月中。终藏之气至此而极也。」)
>
> — **Wu Cheng**, ***Monthly Ordinances of the Seventy-Two Pentads*** (《月令七十二候集解》, Yuan dynasty)

## I. Yin at Its Utmost — the First Yang Returns

Each year between 21 and 23 December on the Gregorian calendar, when the sun reaches celestial longitude 270°, **Winter Solstice** (*Dongzhi*, 冬至) arrives. In the northern hemisphere it is the day of shortest daylight and longest night; once it is past, the daylight comes back inch by inch. The ancients set up a gnomon and measured its shadow, fixing the day of the year's longest shadow as the solstice — of the twenty-four solar terms, the nodes by which the traditional Chinese calendar divides the sun's yearly round, this was the first to be determined.

The three pentads — the five-day phases into which each solar term is further divided — are all written quietly. First pentad, "the earthworms knot" (蚯蚓结): "when the six yin months have carried the cold to its extreme, earthworms knot together crosswise, like ropes" — the yang qi, the year's warm active principle, is on the point of stirring yet has not stirred, and so the worms coil and knot. Second pentad, "the mi deer sheds its antlers" (麋角解): the *Monthly Ordinances* leaves its explanation under the Summer Solstice entry — the deer (*lu*) belongs to yang and the mi (the Père David's deer of the old marshlands) to yin; at Summer Solstice, when a thread of yin is born, the deer sheds its antlers; at Winter Solstice, when a thread of yang is born, the mi sheds its antlers, the two mirror images of each other. Third pentad, "the springs stir" (水泉动): "water is born of heaven's first yang — the primal yang that, by the old reckoning, gives birth to water; yang is born, and so it moves" — the springheads are waking quietly beneath the ice.

What happens underground cannot be seen with the naked eye, so the ancients spoke of it in hexagrams: at Winter Solstice a single yang line is born beneath five yin lines — the Fu ("Return") hexagram of the *Yijing* (《周易》). The *Xiang* ("Images") commentary on it reads: "Thunder lodges in the midst of the earth: Return. Thus the former kings closed the passes at the solstice; merchants and travelers did not go about, and the sovereign did not inspect the lands." Merchants stopped traveling and rulers suspended their tours — all to guard the faint yang just now budding.

For precisely this reason, Winter Solstice was a great festival in ancient times. The *Book of the Later Han* (《后汉书》, the history of the Eastern Han dynasty), in its "Treatise on Rites," records: "In the days before and after Winter Solstice, gentlemen settle their bodies and keep still; the hundred officials cease their affairs, government is not conducted, and business is taken up again only once an auspicious day has been chosen." Liu Zhao's commentary to that treatise also preserves a lost passage of the *Book of Han* (《汉书》, the history of the preceding Western Han): "At Winter Solstice the yang qi arises; the way of the ruler lengthens; therefore one offers congratulations." The second line cannot be found in the *Book of Han* as it stands today — it survives only because the commentary kept it — and from that alone one can see how vigorously the Han greeted the winter solstice.

## II. Dumplings, Winter Solstice Balls, and a Course of Tonic Paste

For the foods of Winter Solstice, north and south each have a folk saying to anchor the day. The north says: "If no bowl of dumplings is set out at Winter Solstice, no one will care when the frost nips off your ears." Jiangnan (the region south of the lower Yangtze) and Fujian and Guangdong say: "Eat a tangyuan and grow a year older" — tangyuan being soft balls of glutinous rice. Families in Fujian and Taiwan roll "Winter Solstice balls" (*dongjie yuan*) in red and white, offer them first to the ancestors, and only then does the whole family share them.

The dumpling comes trailing a legend of **Zhang Zhongjing**, the great physician of the late Han. The story runs that when he retired from office and returned to his hometown in the depths of winter, he found villagers along both banks of the Bai River with ears frostbitten raw, so he raised a shed and dispensed a remedy: mutton and a few cold-dispelling herbs chopped fine, wrapped in dough skin folded into the shape of an ear, boiled and served out with the broth. He called it the **Cold-Dispelling Tender-Ear Decoction** (*Quhan Jiao'er Tang*, 祛寒娇耳汤), and kept serving it from Winter Solstice all the way to the eve of the lunar New Year. But the story must be told for what it is: purely folk oral tradition, not established medical history. The *Shanghan Zabing Lun* (《伤寒杂病论》, his *Treatise on Cold Damage and Miscellaneous Diseases*) contains no such formula, and no chain of evidence links the "jiao'er" of the tale to today's jiaozi dumplings. A legend was never built to stand up to textual proof — hanging the custom of eating dumplings on a physician reads like the people's own spontaneous tribute.

For households that keep to the old ways, these days bring another affair in Jiangnan: having a tonic paste drawn up. Taking pastes in winter is an old tradition of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Shanghai region — the gao fang (膏方) are concentrated herbal formulas, simmered down with honey or syrup into a thick paste and taken by the spoonful. The hospitals of traditional Chinese medicine and the time-honored pharmacies hold consultations around Beginning of Winter (Lidong), the paste is started at Winter Solstice, and one course lasts the whole winter. Its logic differs from that of a decoction: the physician looks, listens and smells, asks, and takes the pulse before writing the prescription, and **one person, one formula** — nothing is shared around. The paste acts slowly and gently, seeking no quick effect, tonifying little by little — just the principle that a lingering condition is treated at its root. The *Suwen* (《素问》, the foundational canon of Chinese medicine), in its "Discourse on the Sincere Words of the Golden Cabinet," says: "Essence is the root of the body. One who has stored essence well will not fall ill with the fevers of spring." To store essence in winter is to bank next spring's capital.

One thing to say first: the tonic paste is medicine, not a byword for "health supplements." Those with a cold and fever, those in an acute flare-up of illness, and those with a thick, greasy tongue coating and heavy damp should treat first and tonify later; those whose spleen and stomach — in Chinese-medicine terms, the digestive core — are weak should settle their appetite first and start the paste after. The formula must be individually drawn up by a qualified Chinese-medicine physician at a face-to-face consultation: do not copy someone else's, and do not put faith in expensive ingredients piled one on another — what matches your condition is what is good.

## III. Daily Life: Rest the Body, from the First Nine to the Ninth

In the Han, even the government shut its offices for the solstice; the least we can do today is loosen the reins on our schedules a little. Skip the sweat-drenched workouts these few days: exercise only until the body feels warm, then stop — once the sweat runs, the faint newborn yang drains away with it. After lunch, lean back against the chair and doze for twenty minutes; that beats slumping over the desk edge with your arms pinned beneath you.

The counting of the nines (*shu jiu*) — nine nine-day periods counted off from the solstice to mark winter's depth and turn — also begins on the day of Winter Solstice itself. The folk rhyme runs: "In the first and second nines, hands stay tucked away; in the third and fourth, walk out upon the ice; in the fifth and sixth, watch the willows along the river; in the seventh the rivers open, in the eighth the wild geese come; add nine more days to the ninth nine, and plough oxen are everywhere in the fields." The versions handed down in different regions differ in small ways, but "the cold peaks in the third nine" is everyone's shared experience — do not brace against the cold only for these few days; the hard fighting comes after New Year's Day. As for the gloves and scarves the first two nines call for: in place one day early, one day more at ease.

## IV. The Mind: Gathering Round the Stove Is Also a Way of Storing

The Return hexagram counsels "closing the passes"; brought down to today, it means simply: get home early.

In the season when the first yang is born, stillness and storing are in order: liveliness is warmth flung outward, togetherness is warmth gathered in. On a snowy evening Bai Juyi wrote to a friend: "New wine, green with its unskimmed froth; a little stove of red clay. Toward evening the sky is threatening snow — could you drink a cup with me?" Twenty Chinese characters, and every one of them a Chinese invitation to winter — new wine, a small stove, a snow-night still to come, and one friend who simply must come.

Du Fu, writing on the solstice, said: "The seasons and human affairs hasten each other on day by day; at Winter Solstice the yang is born and spring comes again" — though he wrote it in Kuizhou, a remote town up the Yangtze gorges, he could still see that thread of yang qi. So can we. On the night of Winter Solstice, wrap up what is in hand and get home early; when the pot boils and the dumplings float up, sit down around the table, the whole family together. What the classics call nourishing what is stored begins with nothing grander than getting everyone to one hot meal.

## V. Four Foods of the Season

**Napa cabbage.** The winter-stored cabbage of the north is at its prime now, thick-ribbed and tight-hearted. Stir-fried with vinegar it is tart and appetizing; stewed with tofu and glass noodles, it makes the least fussy of home-style warming pots.

**Winter bamboo shoots.** In season in Jiangnan: peel and slice, blanch first to draw out the astringency, then braise in oil or stew with cured pork. The fiber is coarse; anyone with a weak stomach should take no more than a taste.

**Water chestnuts.** Their clean sweetness suits the dry days of winter: peel and boil them in water for a drink, or chop them fine into meatball filling. They grow in water, so they must be cooked through before eating — never raw.

**Sugarcane.** Bundled cane comes to market around the solstice; cut into sections and boiled, its water generates fluids and moistens dryness. Its nature leans cool; those with a weak, cold spleen and stomach should eat little.
